The Sample Size Calculator estimates how many observations are needed to estimate a population proportion. Enter a confidence level, margin of error, expected proportion, and optionally the total population size to calculate the required sample size.

What Is the Sample Size Calculator?

A Sample Size Calculator helps plan a survey or study when the goal is to estimate a population proportion with a chosen level of confidence and precision.

It uses the expected proportion and margin of error to calculate an initial sample size. If a finite population size is entered, the calculator applies a finite-population correction that can reduce the required sample.

How to Use the Sample Size Calculator

Enter the following values in the calculator:

  1. Confidence level: The desired confidence level for the estimate. It determines the standard normal critical value, zz, used in the formula.
  2. Margin of error (%): The maximum desired difference between the sample estimate and the population proportion. The percentage is converted to decimal form for the calculation.
  3. Expected proportion (%): The proportion you expect in the population. It is converted from a percentage to a decimal pp. If there is no reliable prior estimate, 50%50\% is commonly used because p(1−p)p(1-p) is largest at p=0.5p=0.5, producing the most conservative sample size.
  4. Population size (optional): The total number of individuals or units in the population. When provided, it is used for the finite-population correction.

Formula / Calculation Method

Main Formula

For a large or unspecified population, the calculator first calculates:

n0=z2p(1−p)e2n_0=\frac{z^2p(1-p)}{e^2}

This is the standard normal-approximation sample-size formula for estimating a population proportion.

When a finite population size is entered, the calculator applies:

n=n01+n0−1Nn=\frac{n_0}{1+\frac{n_0-1}{N}}

This adjustment reduces the required sample when the sample would represent a meaningful fraction of the total population. The equivalent finite-population formulation is also derived in Penn State's survey-sampling material.

Calculation Sequence

  1. Convert the margin of error from a percentage to a decimal.
  2. Convert the expected proportion from a percentage to a decimal.
  3. Determine the normal critical value zz from the confidence level.
  4. Calculate the initial sample size n0n_0.
  5. If population size NN is supplied, apply the finite-population correction.
  6. Interpret the result as the number of observations required for the stated assumptions.

Because a study cannot collect a fraction of an observation, a fractional minimum sample requirement is normally rounded upward when planning the final sample. Penn State's examples likewise round required sample sizes upward.

Worked Example

Suppose you want to estimate a population proportion using these values:

Inputs

  • Margin of error: 5%
  • Expected proportion: 50%
  • Population size: 10,000

For a 95% confidence level, use z=1.96z=1.96.

Convert the percentages:

Step 2: Apply the Finite-Population Correction

Final Result

The calculated requirement is approximately 369.98 observations, so a whole-number planning target is 370 observations.

Without the finite-population adjustment, the initial calculation would be approximately 384.16 observations.

Understanding Your Results

The main result is the estimated number of observations required to estimate one population proportion under the selected confidence level, margin of error, expected proportion, and population-size assumptions.

A smaller margin of error generally requires a larger sample because the estimate must be more precise.

A higher confidence level generally increases the required sample because a larger critical value is used.

The expected proportion also affects sample size. The quantity p(1−p)p(1-p) reaches its maximum at p=0.5p=0.5, which is why a 50% expected proportion produces the largest sample requirement when other inputs remain unchanged.

When population size is supplied, the finite-population correction may lower the sample requirement. Its effect becomes more important when the sample is not small relative to the entire population.

Assumptions and Limitations

This calculator is intended for planning the sample size needed to estimate a single population proportion using a normal-approximation method.

The calculation assumes a sampling situation compatible with simple random sampling. More complex designs may require a different method or a larger effective sample.

The basic calculation does not independently account for clustering, unequal sampling weights, stratification effects, anticipated nonresponse, repeated measurements, subgroup-specific precision, or design effects.

It is also different from a statistical-power calculation for hypothesis testing. Studies designed to detect a particular difference between groups normally require additional inputs such as effect size and statistical power.

For very rare or very common outcomes, small samples, or situations where the normal approximation is unsuitable, a study-specific statistical method may be more appropriate.

The calculated sample size describes the number of usable observations needed under the stated assumptions. If some invited participants are expected not to respond or provide usable data, the number of people contacted may need to be higher.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does a sample size calculator calculate?

It estimates the number of observations needed to estimate a population proportion for a chosen confidence level, margin of error, expected proportion, and optional finite population size.

Why is 50% often used for the expected proportion?

For the proportion formula, p(1−p)p(1-p) is largest when p=0.5p=0.5. Using 50% therefore gives the largest, most conservative sample-size estimate when no better prior estimate is available.

Does a smaller margin of error require a larger sample?

Yes. Margin of error appears squared in the denominator of the formula, so reducing the desired margin of error can substantially increase the required sample.

Why does population size sometimes reduce the sample size?

When the population is finite, sampling a substantial portion of that population provides more information than the large-population formula assumes. The finite-population correction accounts for this relationship.

Do I always need to enter population size?

No. Population size is optional for this calculator. If it is not supplied, the initial large-population formula n0n_0 is used without the finite-population adjustment.

Should the calculated sample size be rounded up?

When the formula gives a fractional minimum requirement, statistical sample-size planning normally rounds upward so the collected sample is not smaller than the calculated requirement.

Is this calculator the same as a statistical power calculator?

No. This calculator estimates sample size for estimating one proportion with a specified confidence level and margin of error. Power calculations for hypothesis tests require additional assumptions about the effect being detected and the desired statistical power.
