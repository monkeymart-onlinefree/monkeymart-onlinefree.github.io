Use the Rental Property Calculator to analyze a financed rental property using the purchase price, mortgage terms, rental income, vacancy, operating expenses, growth assumptions, and expected sale costs. It estimates first-year net operating income (NOI), cash flow, cap rate, cash-on-cash return, initial cash invested, projected sale price, and long-term investment performance.

What Is the Rental Property Calculator?

The Rental Property Calculator estimates the financial performance of a rental property from purchase through a future sale. It combines property income and operating expenses with mortgage financing so you can compare property-level performance, such as NOI and cap rate, with financing-dependent measures such as cash flow and cash-on-cash return.

The calculator is useful for analyzing a potential rental purchase, testing different rent or expense assumptions, and seeing how financing, vacancy, appreciation, and selling costs may affect projected results.

How to Use the Rental Property Calculator

Enter the following values in the calculator:

  • Purchase Price: The price paid for the rental property.
  • Down Payment (%): The percentage of the purchase price paid upfront rather than financed.
  • Mortgage Rate (%): The annual interest rate on the mortgage.
  • Loan Term (years): The number of years used to repay the mortgage.
  • Closing + Repair Cash: Additional upfront cash for closing costs and repairs.
  • Monthly Rent: Expected monthly rental income.
  • Other Monthly Income: Additional recurring property income.
  • Vacancy Rate (%): The percentage of potential rental income expected to be lost because of vacancy.
  • Management Fee (%): The management percentage charged on collected income after vacancy.
  • Property Tax / year: Estimated annual property tax.
  • Insurance / year: Annual property insurance expense.
  • HOA / year: Annual homeowners association fees.
  • Maintenance / year: Expected annual maintenance expense.
  • Other Operating Expenses / year: Other recurring operating expenses included in the analysis.
  • Rent Growth (%/year): Expected annual growth in property income.
  • Expense Growth (%/year): Expected annual growth in operating expenses.
  • Property Appreciation (%/year): Expected annual change in property value.
  • Holding Period (years): How long the property is expected to be held before sale.
  • Selling Cost (%): Selling costs expressed as a percentage of the projected sale price.

The calculator uses these inputs only; it does not automatically add tax, depreciation, refinancing, or other assumptions that are not entered.

Formula / Calculation Method

The calculator projects annual rental-property cash flows, calculates mortgage financing, estimates a future sale, and uses the resulting cash-flow series to evaluate the investment.

Main Variables

Let:

  • P = purchase price
  • d = down payment percentage as a decimal
  • L = mortgage loan amount
  • r = annual mortgage interest rate
  • i = monthly mortgage rate
  • n = total number of monthly mortgage payments
  • M = monthly principal-and-interest payment
  • R = monthly rent
  • O = other monthly income
  • v = vacancy rate
  • m = management fee rate
  • E₁ = first-year operating expenses
  • gᵣ = annual income growth rate
  • gₑ = annual expense growth rate
  • a = annual property appreciation rate
  • H = holding period in years
  • s = selling cost rate
  • C₀ = initial cash invested

Percentages are converted to decimal form before calculations are performed.

1. Mortgage Amount

The financed amount is:

L = P × (1 − d)

The down payment amount is:

Down Payment = P × d

2. Monthly Mortgage Payment

For a fixed-rate mortgage with a positive interest rate:

M = L × [i(1 + i)ⁿ] ÷ [(1 + i)ⁿ − 1]

where:

i = r ÷ 12

and:

n = Loan Term × 12

If the entered mortgage rate is zero, the payment can be calculated as the loan amount divided by the number of monthly payments.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains that fixed-rate principal-and-interest payments depend on the loan amount, loan term, and interest rate.

3. Initial Cash Invested

Initial cash invested includes the down payment plus the entered closing and repair cash:

C₀ = Down Payment + Closing and Repair Cash

This amount is separate from the financed mortgage balance.

4. Annual Rental Income

For year y, projected gross property income is based on monthly rent, other monthly income, and the entered annual income-growth assumption:

Gross Incomeᵧ = 12 × (R + O) × (1 + gᵣ)ʸ⁻¹

5. Vacancy Adjustment

Income after vacancy is:

Collected Incomeᵧ = Gross Incomeᵧ × (1 − v)

6. Management Fee

The calculator applies the management fee to collected income:

Management Feeᵧ = Collected Incomeᵧ × m

This means the fee is calculated after the vacancy adjustment rather than on the full scheduled income.

7. Operating Expenses

First-year operating expenses are the sum of the entered annual expenses:

E₁ = Property Tax + Insurance + HOA + Maintenance + Other Operating Expenses

Future operating expenses are projected using the entered annual expense growth rate:

Expensesᵧ = E₁ × (1 + gₑ)ʸ⁻¹

8. Net Operating Income

Net operating income is property income after vacancy, management fees, and operating expenses, but before mortgage payments:

NOIᵧ = Collected Incomeᵧ − Management Feeᵧ − Expensesᵧ

Mortgage principal and interest are not deducted when calculating NOI.

9. First-Year Cash Flow

Annual mortgage debt service is based on the monthly mortgage payment:

Annual Debt Service = M × 12

First-year cash flow is:

Cash Flow₁ = NOI₁ − Annual Debt Service

For later years, the same structure is used while income and operating expenses change according to the entered growth rates.

10. Capitalization Rate

The calculator uses first-year NOI and purchase price:

Cap Rate = NOI₁ ÷ Purchase Price × 100

Cap rate is calculated before mortgage debt service, so it measures the property's operating performance rather than the effect of financing. This treatment is consistent with the standard NOI-to-value cap-rate structure used in rental-property analysis.

11. Cash-on-Cash Return

Cash-on-cash return compares first-year cash flow with the upfront cash invested:

Cash-on-Cash Return = Cash Flow₁ ÷ Initial Cash Invested × 100

Because mortgage payments affect cash flow, this metric changes when the down payment, mortgage rate, or loan term changes.

12. Projected Sale Price

The estimated property value after the holding period is:

Projected Sale Price = P × (1 + a)ᴴ

The result depends entirely on the appreciation rate entered by the user.

13. Selling Costs

Selling costs are calculated from the projected sale price:

Selling Costs = Projected Sale Price × s

14. Remaining Mortgage Balance

When the property is sold before the loan is fully repaid, the outstanding mortgage balance must be deducted from sale proceeds.

For k monthly payments already made:

Balanceₖ = L(1 + i)ᵏ − M × [(1 + i)ᵏ − 1] ÷ i

The remaining balance approaches zero as the loan reaches the end of its amortization term.

15. Net Sale Proceeds

Net proceeds from the property sale are:

Net Sale Proceeds = Projected Sale Price − Selling Costs − Remaining Mortgage Balance

The net sale proceeds are added to the property's operating cash flow in the final year.

16. Internal Rate of Return

The investment projection begins with the negative initial cash investment, followed by annual operating cash flows. The final year's cash flow also includes net sale proceeds.

IRR is the rate q that makes the net present value of the cash-flow series equal to zero:

0 = −C₀ + CF₁/(1 + q)¹ + CF₂/(1 + q)² + ... + CFᴴ/(1 + q)ᴴ

There is generally no simple direct algebraic solution for IRR, so it is solved numerically. Microsoft documents IRR as an iterative calculation using regularly spaced cash flows containing at least one negative and one positive value.

Calculation Sequence

The calculator follows this general order:

  1. Convert percentage inputs to decimals.
  2. Calculate the down payment and mortgage loan amount.
  3. Calculate the fixed monthly mortgage payment.
  4. Calculate initial cash invested.
  5. Project annual rental and other property income.
  6. Apply vacancy and management fees.
  7. Project annual operating expenses.
  8. Calculate NOI and property cash flow.
  9. Calculate first-year cap rate and cash-on-cash return.
  10. Project property value through the holding period.
  11. Calculate selling costs and remaining mortgage balance.
  12. Add net sale proceeds to the final year's cash flow.
  13. Solve the projected cash-flow series for IRR.

Worked Example

Assume the following rental property:

Inputs

  • Purchase price: $300,000
  • Down payment: 25%
  • Mortgage rate: 6%
  • Loan term: 30 years
  • Closing + repair cash: $15,000
  • Monthly rent: $2,600
  • Other monthly income: $100
  • Vacancy rate: 5%
  • Management fee: 8%
  • Property tax: $3,600/year
  • Insurance: $1,500/year
  • HOA: $0/year
  • Maintenance: $2,400/year
  • Other operating expenses: $600/year
  • Rent growth: 3%/year
  • Expense growth: 3%/year
  • Property appreciation: 3%/year
  • Holding period: 5 years
  • Selling cost: 6%

Step 1: Calculate Financing

Down payment:

$300,000 × 25% = $75,000

Mortgage amount:

$300,000 − $75,000 = $225,000

At 6% interest over 30 years, the calculated monthly principal-and-interest payment is approximately:

$1,348.99

Annual mortgage payments:

$1,348.99 × 12 = $16,187.86

Initial cash invested:

$75,000 + $15,000 = $90,000

Step 2: Calculate First-Year NOI

Gross annual income:

($2,600 + $100) × 12 = $32,400

Income after 5% vacancy:

$32,400 × 95% = $30,780

Management fee:

$30,780 × 8% = $2,462.40

Annual operating expenses:

$3,600 + $1,500 + $0 + $2,400 + $600 = $8,100

First-year NOI:

$30,780 − $2,462.40 − $8,100 = $20,217.60

Step 3: Calculate First-Year Returns

First-year cash flow:

$20,217.60 − $16,187.86 = $4,029.74

Cap rate:

$20,217.60 ÷ $300,000 × 100 ≈ 6.74%

Cash-on-cash return:

$4,029.74 ÷ $90,000 × 100 ≈ 4.48%

Step 4: Project the Sale

Projected value after five years:

$300,000 × 1.03⁵ = $347,782.22

Selling costs:

$347,782.22 × 6% = $20,866.93

After 60 mortgage payments, the remaining loan balance is approximately:

$209,372.30

Estimated net sale proceeds:

$347,782.22 − $20,866.93 − $209,372.30 = $117,542.99

With the example's 3% annual income and expense growth assumptions, the projected annual operating cash flows are approximately:

Year 1: $4,029.74
Year 2: $4,636.26
Year 3: $5,260.99
Year 4: $5,904.45
Year 5: $6,567.22

Adding the estimated net sale proceeds to year five gives a final-year total cash flow of approximately $124,110.21.

Using an initial cash outflow of $90,000 and these projected annual cash flows produces an estimated IRR of approximately 10.67%.

Final Result

For this example:

First-Year NOI: $20,217.60
First-Year Cash Flow: $4,029.74
Cap Rate: 6.74%
Cash-on-Cash Return: 4.48%
Initial Cash Invested: $90,000
Projected Sale Price: $347,782.22
Projected 5-Year IRR: approximately 10.67%

These are estimates based on the entered assumptions, not guaranteed investment results.

Understanding Your Results

First-Year NOI shows the property's income after vacancy, management fees, and operating expenses but before mortgage payments. It helps separate the property's operating performance from its financing structure.

First-Year Cash Flow shows the money remaining after operating expenses and mortgage debt service. A negative result means the modeled property costs more to operate and finance than it generates during that period.

Cap Rate compares first-year NOI with the purchase price. Because mortgage payments are excluded, two buyers using different financing assumptions can still calculate the same cap rate from the same property assumptions.

Cash-on-Cash Return compares first-year cash flow with the amount of cash invested upfront. It is affected by financing because mortgage payments and the size of the down payment change the result.

Initial Cash Invested represents the down payment plus the closing and repair cash entered in the calculator.

Projected Sale Price is the estimated future property value based on the appreciation rate and holding period entered. It is not a market-value guarantee.

IRR considers the timing of the initial investment, annual property cash flows, mortgage financing, and final sale proceeds. Because it includes several long-term assumptions, small changes can have a large effect on the result.

Assumptions and Limitations

Rental-property projections are highly sensitive to assumptions about rent, vacancy, repairs, insurance, taxes, financing costs, property appreciation, and selling costs.

The calculator does not include income taxes, depreciation deductions, capital gains tax, depreciation recapture, refinancing, major irregular capital expenditures, or other cash flows that are not represented by the available inputs.

It assumes annual growth rates continue according to the percentages entered. Actual rent, expenses, vacancy, property values, and selling costs may rise or fall differently.

Mortgage estimates may differ from a lender's actual figures because a real mortgage can include mortgage insurance, escrow requirements, lender fees, points, prepaid costs, or other charges. CFPB guidance notes that the total payment associated with a mortgage can include costs beyond principal and interest, including taxes and insurance.

The model treats its cash flows as estimates and should not be considered tax, legal, lending, or investment advice.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is NOI in a rental property calculation?

Net operating income is the property's collected income after vacancy, management fees, and operating expenses, but before mortgage principal and interest. It measures operating performance independently from the financing structure.

Does cap rate include the mortgage payment?

No. This calculator calculates cap rate from first-year NOI divided by the purchase price. Mortgage debt service is excluded.

How is cash-on-cash return different from cap rate?

Cap rate compares NOI with the property price and ignores financing. Cash-on-cash return compares cash flow after mortgage payments with the amount of cash invested upfront, so financing directly affects it.

How does vacancy affect the calculation?

The vacancy rate reduces projected gross property income before the management fee and other operating expenses are calculated. A higher vacancy assumption therefore lowers NOI and cash flow.

How is the future sale price estimated?

The purchase price is compounded by the annual property appreciation rate for the selected holding period. Selling costs and the outstanding mortgage balance are then deducted when estimating net sale proceeds.

Why can the projected IRR change significantly?

IRR uses the complete cash-flow sequence. Changes in rent growth, vacancy, expenses, mortgage terms, appreciation, holding period, selling costs, or the remaining mortgage balance can therefore change the result substantially.

Does the calculator include depreciation or capital gains tax?

No. Income taxes, depreciation, capital gains tax, and depreciation recapture are outside this calculator's modeled inputs. They should be evaluated separately when relevant.
