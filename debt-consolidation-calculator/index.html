Use the Debt Consolidation Calculator to compare your existing debts with a new consolidation loan. Enter your debt balances, APRs and payments, then add the proposed consolidation APR, loan term and upfront fee to estimate the new monthly payment and overall cost.

What Is a Debt Consolidation Calculator?

A Debt Consolidation Calculator estimates what could happen if you replace multiple existing debts with one new loan.

It compares the combined balance and current payments of your entered debts with a proposed consolidation loan. The results can help you compare the estimated monthly payment, interest and total cost before deciding whether a consolidation offer fits your situation.

Debt consolidation can simplify several debts into one payment, but a lower monthly payment does not always mean a lower overall cost. A longer repayment term or added fees can increase the total amount paid.

How to Use the Debt Consolidation Calculator

For each debt you want to consolidate, enter its:

  1. Balance ($): The amount currently owed.
  2. APR (%): The annual percentage rate charged on that debt.
  3. Current payment ($): The regular payment used by the calculator to estimate the debt's remaining payoff cost.

Then enter the proposed consolidation-loan details:

  1. Consolidation APR (%): The annual rate for the new loan.
  2. New term (years): How long you will take to repay the consolidation loan.
  3. Origination / upfront fee ($): A fixed fee associated with obtaining the new loan.

Use the units shown in the calculator and follow its built-in field validation for entry limits.

Formula / Calculation Method

Consolidation Loan Payment Formula

The new estimated monthly payment uses the standard fixed-payment amortized-loan formula:

M=P×r(1+r)n(1+r)n−1M=P\times\frac{r(1+r)^n}{(1+r)^n-1}

Where:

  • M = estimated monthly consolidation payment, in dollars
  • P = total balance being consolidated, in dollars
  • r = monthly interest rate
  • n = total number of monthly payments

The monthly rate is:

r=APR/10012r=\frac{APR/100}{12}

The number of payments is:

n=Loan term in years×12n=\text{Loan term in years}\times12

This is the standard fixed-payment amortization method used for installment loans.

Total Balance Consolidated

The calculator adds the balances of all entered debts:

P=B1+B2+⋯+BkP=B_1+B_2+\dots+B_k

where each B represents an existing debt balance.

Current Monthly Payments

Current monthly payments are combined:

Current Monthly Payments=PMT1+PMT2+⋯+PMTkCurrent\ Monthly\ Payments=PMT_1+PMT_2+\dots+PMT_k

Existing Debt Payoff Method

Each existing debt is treated as a fixed-payment amortizing balance using its entered APR and current payment.

For a monthly period:

Monthly Rate=APR/10012Monthly\ Rate=\frac{APR/100}{12} Interest=Opening Balance×Monthly RateInterest=Opening\ Balance\times Monthly\ Rate Principal Paid=Payment−InterestPrincipal\ Paid=Payment-Interest New Balance=Opening Balance−Principal PaidNew\ Balance=Opening\ Balance-Principal\ Paid

The process continues until the modeled balance is paid. The estimated costs for the individual debts are then combined to produce the Estimated Current Payoff Cost.

Actual credit cards may not behave this way because minimum payments can change as balances change.

Estimated Consolidation Interest

After calculating the new monthly payment:

Total Scheduled Payments=M×nTotal\ Scheduled\ Payments=M\times n Estimated Consolidation Interest=(M×n)−PEstimated\ Consolidation\ Interest=(M\times n)-P

New Total Cost Including Fee

The calculator includes the entered upfront fee in the cost comparison:

New Total Cost=(M×n)+FeeNew\ Total\ Cost=(M\times n)+Fee

Estimated Cost Difference

The calculator compares the New Total Cost Including Fee with the Estimated Current Payoff Cost and reports the difference between the two modeled costs.

This makes it possible to compare total cost rather than looking only at the monthly payment.

Worked Example

Suppose two debts are being considered for consolidation.

Inputs

Existing Debt 1

  • Balance: $6,009.12
  • APR: 18%
  • Current payment: $300 per month

Existing Debt 2

  • Balance: $4,248.67
  • APR: 12%
  • Current payment: $200 per month

Proposed Consolidation Loan

  • Consolidation APR: 10%
  • Term: 2 years
  • Upfront fee: $250

Step 1: Calculate the Total Balance

P=6,009.12+4,248.67P=6,009.12+4,248.67 P=10,257.79P=10,257.79

The total balance consolidated is $10,257.79.

The current monthly payments total:

300+200=500300+200=500

So the current monthly payment amount is $500.

Step 2: Convert the New APR and Term

Monthly rate:

r=0.1012=0.00833333r=\frac{0.10}{12}=0.00833333

Number of payments:

n=2×12=24n=2\times12=24

Step 3: Calculate the New Monthly Payment

M=10,257.79×0.00833333(1.00833333)24(1.00833333)24−1M=10,257.79\times \frac{0.00833333(1.00833333)^{24}} {(1.00833333)^{24}-1} M≈473.34496M\approx473.34496

The estimated new monthly payment is approximately $473.34.

Step 4: Calculate Consolidation Interest

Using the unrounded payment during the calculation:

Total Scheduled Payments≈473.34496×24Total\ Scheduled\ Payments\approx473.34496\times24 ≈11,360.28\approx11,360.28

Estimated interest:

11,360.28−10,257.79=1,102.4911,360.28-10,257.79=1,102.49

The estimated consolidation interest is approximately $1,102.49.

Step 5: Include the Upfront Fee

11,360.28+250=11,610.2811,360.28+250=11,610.28

The new estimated total cost including the fee is approximately $11,610.28.

Under the calculator's fixed-payment model, the two existing debts have an estimated combined payoff cost of about $11,999.99.

The two modeled costs therefore differ by approximately:

11,999.99−11,610.28=389.7111,999.99-11,610.28=389.71

In this example, the consolidation loan is approximately $389.71 lower in modeled total cost, while reducing the combined monthly payment from $500 to about $473.34.

Understanding Your Results

New Estimated Monthly Payment

This is the calculated monthly payment required to repay the consolidated balance over the selected term at the entered consolidation APR.

A lower payment by itself does not prove that consolidation costs less overall. Extending the repayment period can lower the monthly payment while increasing total interest. CFPB specifically advises consumers to compare the term, fees and total cost rather than focusing only on the monthly payment.

Total Balance Consolidated

This is the sum of the balances entered for the existing debts. It becomes the principal used for the consolidation-loan payment calculation.

Current Monthly Payments

This is the combined amount of the current payments entered for all debts.

It can be compared with the new estimated monthly payment to see how consolidation could change the modeled monthly payment burden.

Estimated Consolidation Interest

This is the difference between the scheduled consolidation-loan payments and the balance being consolidated.

It does not include the separate upfront fee.

New Total Cost Including Fee

This combines all scheduled payments on the consolidation loan with the entered origination or upfront fee.

Fees can materially affect the real cost of an installment loan, and origination fees are among the fees that may apply to personal installment loans.

Estimated Current Payoff Cost

This estimates how much the entered debts would cost to repay if their entered APRs and fixed payments continued under the calculator's amortization model.

It is an estimate rather than a lender payoff quote.

Estimated Cost Difference

This shows the gap between the modeled current payoff cost and the modeled consolidation cost including the entered fee.

Use this figure together with the monthly-payment comparison rather than evaluating either result alone.

Assumptions and Limitations

The calculator models each existing debt as a fixed-payment amortizing balance using the APR and payment you enter.

Real credit cards may use changing minimum-payment formulas, variable APRs or other account-specific rules. This means the estimated current payoff cost can differ from actual statements or creditor payoff figures.

The consolidation calculation uses the entered consolidation APR and term. An actual lender offer may have different rates, fees, eligibility requirements or loan conditions.

The entered upfront fee is included when comparing total costs. Other charges that are not entered into the calculator are not represented in the result.

Debt consolidation can also have effects that a mathematical calculator cannot measure, including credit effects, collateral risk and borrower eligibility. For example, using home equity for consolidation can put the home at risk if the resulting loan is not repaid.

Results are estimates for comparison purposes. They are not a guarantee of loan approval, lender terms or savings.

Frequently Asked Questions

How does the Debt Consolidation Calculator calculate the new payment?

It uses the fixed-payment amortization formula based on the total consolidated balance, monthly interest rate and number of monthly payments.

Does a lower consolidation payment mean I will save money?

Not necessarily. A longer term can reduce the monthly payment while increasing the amount paid over time. Fees can also reduce or eliminate potential savings.

Why does the calculator ask for my current debt payments?

The current payments are used with each debt's balance and APR to estimate how much the existing debts may cost to pay off under a fixed-payment model.

Are origination fees included?

Yes. The calculator includes the entered origination or upfront fee when calculating the new total cost used for comparison.

Why can the estimated current payoff cost differ from my credit card statement?

Credit card minimum payments often change as the balance changes. Actual accounts can also have different interest calculations, fees and account terms, while this calculator uses a simplified fixed-payment model.

What happens if a current payment is too low to reduce the debt?

For an amortizing debt, the payment must be sufficient to cover the interest being added and reduce principal. If a payment does not reduce the balance, a normal finite payoff estimate may not be possible under the fixed-payment model.

Is debt consolidation always cheaper than keeping separate debts?

No. The result depends on the consolidation APR, term, upfront fee and the cost of continuing your existing debts. CFPB notes that consolidation can cost more overall when fees or longer repayment periods are involved.
