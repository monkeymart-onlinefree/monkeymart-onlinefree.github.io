The Rent vs Buy Calculator compares the financial outcome of renting a home with buying one over the number of years you expect to stay. It uses your rent, home price, mortgage terms, ownership costs, appreciation, transaction costs, and investment-return assumptions to compare the buyer’s ending home equity with the renter’s ending invested wealth.

What Is the Rent vs Buy Calculator?

A rent vs buy calculator helps compare two different housing paths using the same time horizon and financial assumptions.

For the buying path, the calculator models a down payment, buying closing costs, an amortizing mortgage, property tax, insurance, maintenance, home-price appreciation, selling costs, and the remaining mortgage balance when the home is sold.

For the renting path, it models monthly rent and rent growth while investing the cash that would otherwise have been used for the buyer’s down payment and closing costs. Monthly cost differences between buying and renting are also included in the renter-side investment path.

The result is a long-term wealth comparison rather than a simple comparison of rent against a mortgage payment.

How to Use the Rent vs Buy Calculator

Enter the assumptions that describe the home, mortgage, rental alternative, and expected holding period.

Input What to Enter

Rent increase (%/yr) The expected annual percentage increase in rent.
Home price The purchase price of the home.
Down payment (%) The percentage of the home price paid upfront.
Mortgage rate (%/yr) The annual mortgage interest rate.
Mortgage term (years) The full repayment term of the mortgage.

Use estimates that reflect the property and financing scenario you actually want to compare. Actual mortgage charges, taxes, insurance, and closing costs can differ by property, lender, loan product, and location. CFPB guidance also notes that a mortgage payment can include costs beyond principal and interest, particularly taxes and insurance.

Formula / Calculation Method

The calculator uses a month-by-month cash-flow simulation. It calculates the mortgage balance over time, ownership expenses, estimated home value, selling costs, and renter investment balance before comparing ending wealth.

Mortgage Principal

If:

H = home price
d = down payment as a decimal

then:

Down Payment = H × d

and:

Loan Amount (L) = H − (H × d)

or:

L = H × (1 − d)

Monthly Mortgage Payment

For a fixed-rate amortizing mortgage:

i = j ÷ 12
n = T × 12

where:

j = annual mortgage rate as a decimal
i = monthly mortgage rate
T = mortgage term in years
n = total number of monthly payments

The monthly principal-and-interest payment is:

M = L × [i(1 + i)^n] ÷ [(1 + i)^n − 1]

If the mortgage rate is zero:

M = L ÷ n

CFPB explains that a typical fixed-rate mortgage uses a standard payment calculation designed to repay the loan by the end of its stated term.

Mortgage Balance Each Month

The remaining loan balance is updated through amortization.

For month t:

Interestₜ = Balanceₜ₋₁ × i
Principalₜ = M − Interestₜ
Balanceₜ = Balanceₜ₋₁ − Principalₜ

As the balance falls, less of each fixed payment goes to interest and more goes toward principal.

Buyer Upfront Cash

The buyer initially uses cash for the down payment and purchasing costs.

If:

cᵦ = buying closing-cost percentage

then:

Buying Closing Costs = H × cᵦ
Buyer Upfront Cash = Down Payment + Buying Closing Costs

This same amount forms the renter’s initial investable cash in the comparison.

Monthly Buyer Cost

The buyer’s monthly cash outflow combines the amortizing mortgage payment with the ownership expenses included by this calculator:

Buyer Costₜ = Mortgage Payment + Property Taxₜ + Insuranceₜ + Maintenanceₜ

These costs are kept separate from the home’s ending equity because mortgage principal reduces the outstanding loan balance rather than disappearing as a pure expense.

Home Value

The home starts at the entered purchase price and changes over the simulation according to the annual home-appreciation assumption.

If Vₜ is the estimated home value during the simulation, the calculator updates that value across the monthly timeline using the appreciation assumption entered by the user.

The appreciation percentage is a scenario assumption, not a prediction of the property’s actual future value.

Ending Buyer Equity

At the end of the selected holding period, the model treats the home as if it were sold.

If:

V = estimated sale value
s = selling-cost percentage
B = remaining mortgage balance

then:

Selling Costs = V × s

and:

Buyer Ending Equity = V − Selling Costs − B

This represents the estimated amount remaining from the property after selling costs and the outstanding mortgage are removed.

Renter Investment

The renter starts with the cash the buyer would have used for the down payment and buying closing costs:

Initial Renter Investment = Down Payment + Buying Closing Costs

Rent then follows the entered starting monthly rent and annual rent-growth assumption.

For each month, the simulation compares the buyer’s modeled housing outflow with the renter’s rent:

Cash-Flow Differenceₜ = Buyer Costₜ − Rentₜ

That difference is applied to the renter-side investment path, while the accumulated balance grows using the entered investment-return assumption.

This opportunity-cost treatment is important because the comparison does not assume that the renter simply spends the buyer’s avoided upfront cash.

Final Comparison

At the end of the holding period:

Buyer Wealth = Ending Home Equity
Renter Wealth = Ending Renter Investment Balance

The comparison is:

Wealth Difference = Buyer Wealth − Renter Wealth

A positive difference means the buyer path has the higher modeled ending wealth. A negative difference means the renter path has the higher modeled ending wealth.

The result depends entirely on the assumptions entered and should not be treated as a forecast or guaranteed financial outcome.

Worked Example

The following example uses zero home appreciation, zero rent growth, and zero investment return. Keeping these three assumptions at zero makes the example easy to audit while still demonstrating the calculator’s mortgage, transaction-cost, ownership-cost, and wealth-comparison method.

Inputs

Input Example Value
Monthly rent$2,000
Rent increase0%
Home price$300,000
Down payment20%
Mortgage rate6%
Mortgage term30 years
Years in home5 years
Property tax1% per year
Insurance0.3% per year
Maintenance1% per year
Home appreciation0%
Investment return0%
Buy closing costs3%
Selling costs6%

Step 1: Calculate the Loan

Down payment:

$300,000 × 20% = $60,000

Mortgage principal:

$300,000 − $60,000 = $240,000

Buying closing costs:

$300,000 × 3% = $9,000

Total upfront buyer cash:

$60,000 + $9,000 = $69,000

The renter therefore begins the comparison with $69,000 available on the investment side.

Step 2: Calculate the Mortgage Payment

Monthly mortgage rate:

6% ÷ 12 = 0.5% = 0.005

Number of payments:

30 × 12 = 360

Using the fixed-rate mortgage formula:

M ≈ $1,438.92 per month

Step 3: Add Monthly Ownership Costs

Monthly property tax:

$300,000 × 1% ÷ 12 = $250

Monthly insurance:

$300,000 × 0.3% ÷ 12 = $75

Monthly maintenance:

$300,000 × 1% ÷ 12 = $250

Total modeled buyer outflow:

$1,438.92 + $250 + $75 + $250 = $2,013.92 per month

Compared with $2,000 rent, the renter has a monthly cash-flow difference of approximately:

$2,013.92 − $2,000 = $13.92

Because the example assumes a 0% investment return, after 60 months the renter-side wealth is approximately:

69,000+(13.92 × 60) = $69,835.28

using full precision before final rounding.

Step 4: Calculate the Buyer’s Position After Five Years

After 60 mortgage payments, the estimated remaining mortgage balance is approximately:

$223,330.46

With 0% home appreciation, the sale value remains:

$300,000

Selling costs:

$300,000 × 6% = $18,000

Buyer ending equity:

$300,000 − $18,000 − $223,330.46 = $58,669.54

Final Result

Buyer ending wealth:

$58,669.54

Renter ending wealth:

$69,835.28

Difference:

$58,669.54 − 69,835.28=-11,165.74

Under these specific assumptions, the renter finishes the five-year period with approximately $11,166 more modeled wealth than the buyer.

Changing the holding period, appreciation, rent growth, mortgage rate, investment return, or transaction costs can materially change the comparison.

Understanding Your Results

The main result compares the estimated wealth remaining at the end of the selected period rather than simply asking which housing payment is lower.

Buyer ending equity represents the estimated sale value remaining after selling costs and the outstanding mortgage balance.

Renter ending wealth represents the avoided buyer upfront cash plus the effect of monthly buying-versus-renting cash-flow differences and the entered investment return.

Remaining mortgage balance shows how much of the original mortgage would still need to be repaid when the property is sold.

Estimated sale value reflects the home price after applying the appreciation assumption over the selected holding period.

The result is best used for scenario testing. Try more conservative and more optimistic assumptions instead of relying on a single set of percentages.

Assumptions and Limitations

This calculator is a financial planning estimate. Freddie Mac similarly describes rent-versus-buy tools as guidance and financial estimates and recommends contacting a lender for precise mortgage calculations.

The model does not include tax deductions or tax credits, mortgage insurance, HOA fees, renovation value, moving costs, rental security deposits, rent concessions, capital-gains taxes, or lender-specific closing details.

Actual closing costs can contain multiple lender, settlement, transfer, prepaid, insurance, and other charges. CFPB advises reviewing the specific Loan Estimate and Closing Disclosure for the real transaction rather than relying only on a general estimate.

Future rent increases, investment returns, home appreciation, taxes, insurance, and maintenance costs are uncertain. Small changes in these assumptions can produce a significantly different long-term result.

The calculator also measures financial outcomes only. It does not assign a dollar value to flexibility, moving plans, control over a property, housing stability, or other personal reasons for renting or owning.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the mortgage payment alone enough to compare renting and buying?

No. A mortgage payment does not represent the entire cost of owning a home. Property taxes, insurance, maintenance, upfront closing costs, selling costs, and the remaining mortgage balance can all affect the comparison. CFPB also distinguishes principal-and-interest payments from the total monthly housing payment.

Why does the calculator include an investment return for renters?

Buying requires upfront cash for the down payment and closing costs. A renter can potentially keep that money invested instead, so ignoring it would leave out an important opportunity cost. The calculator therefore tracks that avoided upfront cash and monthly cash-flow differences.

Why are selling costs included?

A homeowner does not necessarily receive the full market value of the property when it is sold. The calculator subtracts the entered selling-cost percentage before determining the buyer’s ending equity.

Does a higher home appreciation rate always improve the buying result?

Holding everything else constant, a higher assumed future home value increases the value available before selling costs and the remaining mortgage are deducted. Actual appreciation is uncertain, so the percentage should be treated as a scenario assumption rather than a guaranteed return.

How does the mortgage term affect the comparison?

The mortgage term affects the monthly payment and the speed at which principal is repaid. A different term therefore changes both monthly buyer cash flow and the mortgage balance remaining when the home is sold.

Does the calculator include tax benefits of homeownership?

No. This model excludes tax deductions and tax credits. This avoids assuming that a particular user qualifies for a tax benefit or receives a specific tax outcome.

Is the Rent vs Buy Calculator financial advice?

No. It is a scenario-comparison tool based on the numbers you enter. Mortgage terms, transaction costs, property expenses, taxes, and future market results can differ from the assumptions used here.
