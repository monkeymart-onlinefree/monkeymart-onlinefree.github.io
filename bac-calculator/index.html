The BAC Calculator estimates blood alcohol concentration using your sex category used by the model, body weight, time since your first drink, and the number of preset beer, wine, and spirit servings consumed. It uses a simplified Widmark-style calculation and reports an estimated BAC percentage.

Safety note: This calculator cannot measure your actual BAC or tell you whether you are safe to drive. NHTSA states that even small amounts of alcohol can affect driving and that people cannot reliably assess their own BAC or impairment. If you have consumed alcohol, use a sober driver, taxi or ride service, public transport, or stay where you are.

What Is the BAC Calculator?

The BAC Calculator is an educational tool that estimates blood alcohol concentration after alcohol consumption. It converts fixed beer, wine, and spirit servings into grams of ethanol and then applies a simplified Widmark-style formula based on body weight, the model's sex-specific distribution factor, and elapsed time.

BAC estimates are not the same as a breathalyzer or laboratory blood test. NHTSA notes that actual BAC is measured using breath or blood testing and can be affected by factors such as drinking speed, sex, body weight, and food.

How to Use the BAC Calculator

  1. Sex used by model: Select the applicable model category. The calculation uses a Widmark factor of 0.68 for males and 0.55 for females.
  2. Body weight: Enter your weight in kilograms. Body weight must be greater than zero because it is used in the formula denominator.
  3. Hours since first drink: Enter the elapsed time in hours from the beginning of the drinking session.
  4. Beer: Enter the number of 355 mL beer servings at 5% ABV.
  5. Wine: Enter the number of 148 mL wine servings at 12% ABV.
  6. Spirits: Enter the number of 44 mL spirit servings at 40% ABV.
  7. Calculate to see the estimated BAC.

The calculator's fixed drink sizes closely match current U.S. standard-drink references from the CDC.

Formula / Calculation Method

Alcohol in Each Drink

The calculator first converts beverage volume and alcohol by volume, or ABV, into grams of pure ethanol:

Alcohol grams = Volume in mL × ABV as a decimal × 0.789 g/mL

The ethanol density used is approximately 0.789 grams per milliliter. This density value is also used in published Widmark calculations.

For the calculator's preset servings:

Beer

355 × 0.05 × 0.789 = 14.00475 g

Wine

148 × 0.12 × 0.789 = 14.01264 g

Spirits

44 × 0.40 × 0.789 = 13.8864 g

The total alcohol amount is:

A = (Beer servings × 14.00475) + (Wine servings × 14.01264) + (Spirit servings × 13.8864)

Main BAC Formula

The calculator then uses:

BAC% ≈ [A ÷ (r × W)] ÷ 10 − (0.015 × h)

Variables

Variable Meaning Unit
BAC% Estimated blood alcohol concentration Percent
A Total pure alcohol consumed grams
r Widmark distribution factor 0.68 male / 0.55 female
W Body weight kilograms
h Hours since first drink hours
0.015 Fixed BAC reduction used per hour percentage points per hour

Published descriptions of the classic Widmark approach use sex-specific factors around 0.68 for males and 0.55 for females. A fixed reduction of about 0.015 BAC per hour is also commonly used as an average assumption, although actual alcohol elimination varies between individuals.

Calculation Sequence

  1. Convert each preset drink into grams of ethanol.
  2. Multiply the ethanol amount by the number of each drink consumed.
  3. Add the alcohol amounts to calculate total alcohol grams.
  4. Select the model's Widmark factor: 0.68 or 0.55.
  5. Multiply the factor by body weight in kilograms.
  6. Divide total alcohol grams by this value.
  7. Divide by 10 to express the result as BAC percent.
  8. Subtract 0.015 percentage points for each hour since the first drink.

This calculator uses this simplified sequence exactly. Some research versions of the Widmark equation use additional absorption assumptions, different distribution estimates, or different timing adjustments, so they may produce different results.

Worked Example

Suppose the inputs are:

  • Sex used by model: Male
  • Body weight: 80 kg
  • Hours since first drink: 1 hour
  • Beer: 2 servings
  • Wine: 1 serving
  • Spirits: 0 servings

Step 1: Calculate Alcohol From Beer

2 × 355 × 0.05 × 0.789
= 28.0095 g

Step 2: Calculate Alcohol From Wine

1 × 148 × 0.12 × 0.789
= 14.01264 g

Step 3: Calculate Total Alcohol

28.0095 + 14.01264
= 42.02214 g

Step 4: Apply the Widmark-Style Formula

BAC% ≈ [42.02214 ÷ (0.68 × 80)] ÷ 10 − (0.015 × 1)
BAC% ≈ [42.02214 ÷ 54.4] ÷ 10 − 0.015
BAC% ≈ 0.0772466 − 0.015

Final Result

Estimated BAC ≈ 0.06225%

Rounded to three decimal places, the estimate is approximately 0.062%.

This is only a mathematical estimate. It is not a measurement of the person's actual BAC or impairment.

Understanding Your Results

The main result is the calculator's estimated BAC percentage. For example, an estimated value of 0.062% corresponds numerically to about 0.062 grams of alcohol per deciliter of blood when BAC is expressed as a weight-per-volume concentration.

A higher estimate generally results from more alcohol, a lower body weight, or the model's lower distribution factor. More elapsed time lowers the estimate because the calculator subtracts a fixed 0.015 percentage points per hour.

Do not interpret any particular calculator result as proof that driving is safe. NHTSA states that driving ability can be affected at low BAC levels and that self-assessment of impairment is unreliable.

Assumptions and Limitations

This calculator uses fixed serving sizes, fixed ABV values, a fixed ethanol density, two fixed Widmark distribution factors, and a fixed hourly elimination rate. These assumptions make the calculation useful for education but prevent it from reproducing every person's actual BAC.

Actual alcohol concentration can differ because absorption and metabolism vary. Drinking speed, food, body composition, individual physiology, medications and other substances can also affect impairment or alcohol absorption. NHTSA specifically identifies drinking speed, sex, weight, and food as factors affecting BAC.

The simplified calculation also does not reproduce every version of the Widmark equation. Published methods may account for absorption time, alternative body-water estimates, individual elimination rates, or additional physiological variables.

For an actual BAC measurement, breath or blood testing is required.

Current Reference Data

Checked September 24, 2026.

The CDC currently defines a U.S. standard drink as approximately 14 grams of pure alcohol. Its common examples include 12 ounces of 5% beer, 5 ounces of 12% wine, and 1.5 ounces of 40% distilled spirits. These correspond closely to the calculator's 355 mL beer, 148 mL wine, and 44 mL spirits presets.

These serving sizes are reference quantities. Real beverages can contain different serving volumes and ABV percentages, so one actual glass, bottle, can, cocktail, or shot does not always equal one preset serving.

Frequently Asked Questions

What formula does the BAC Calculator use?

It uses a simplified Widmark-style formula:

BAC% ≈ [alcohol grams ÷ (r × body weight kg)] ÷ 10 − 0.015 × hours

Alcohol grams are calculated from the fixed drink volume, ABV, and an ethanol density of approximately 0.789 g/mL.

Why do beer, wine, and spirits contain similar alcohol amounts in this calculator?

The preset volumes and ABV values are designed around approximately equivalent amounts of pure alcohol. CDC standard-drink guidance similarly lists 12 ounces of 5% beer, 5 ounces of 12% wine, and 1.5 ounces of 40% spirits as roughly one standard drink containing about 14 grams of alcohol.

Why does BAC decrease as time increases?

The calculator subtracts 0.015 percentage points per hour as a fixed elimination assumption. NHTSA training materials describe about 0.015 per hour as an average, but individual elimination rates vary.

Does food change BAC?

Food can slow alcohol absorption, according to NHTSA. However, this calculator does not include food as an input, so that effect is not directly modeled.

Can I use this BAC estimate to decide whether I can drive?

No. An online estimate cannot determine whether you are safe to drive. NHTSA states that even small amounts of alcohol can affect driving and recommends not driving after drinking.

Why might my actual BAC differ from the calculator?

The result depends on simplified assumptions about alcohol content, body-water distribution, and elimination. Actual BAC varies with individual physiology, drinking pattern, absorption, food, beverage strength, and other factors. Published Widmark calculations also use different assumptions and formula variants.

Is this result the same as a breathalyzer result?

No. This calculator produces an estimate from entered information. NHTSA states that BAC is measured through breath testing or blood testing.
