Use the Simple Interest Calculator to calculate simple interest from a principal amount, annual interest rate, and time period. The calculator uses the standard simple interest formula I=P×r×tI = P \times r \times t and can also use the formula to solve for principal, rate, or time when the required values are known.

What Is the Simple Interest Calculator?

A Simple Interest Calculator helps determine interest when interest is calculated only from the original principal for the stated time period.

The basic formula is:

I = P × r × t

where the annual percentage rate is converted to decimal form before calculation. This standard formula is also used in mathematical education references such as OpenStax. (OpenStax)

You can use the calculator for straightforward borrowing, lending, or savings estimates when the agreement actually uses simple interest.

How to Use the Simple Interest Calculator

Principal ($): Enter the original amount of money borrowed, invested, or deposited.

Annual Rate (%): Enter the yearly interest rate as a percentage. For example, 5% is converted to 0.05 for the calculation.

Time (Years): Enter the length of time in years. A half-year can be represented as 0.5 years when appropriate.

Interest ($): Enter the known interest amount when using the simple-interest relationship to solve for another variable such as principal, rate, or time.

Use the values relevant to the calculation you want to perform.

Formula / Calculation Method

Main Formula

The calculator uses:

I=P×r×tI = P \times r \times t

The final amount can be calculated as:

A=P+IA = P + I

Variables

Symbol Meaning Unit
I Simple interest Dollars ($)
P Principal or original amount Dollars ($)
r Annual interest rate expressed as a decimal Decimal
t Time Years
A Principal plus simple interest Dollars ($)

OpenStax defines simple interest using the same I=PrtI = Prt relationship, with PP as principal, rr as rate, and tt as time in years. (OpenStax)

Calculation Sequence

  1. Convert the annual percentage rate to decimal form:
    r=Rate %100r = \frac{\text{Rate \\%}}{100}
  2. Multiply the principal by the decimal annual rate.
  3. Multiply that result by the time in years.
  4. The result is the simple interest.
  5. When a final amount is needed, add the interest to the principal.

Solving for Principal

When interest, rate, and time are known:

P=Ir×tP = \frac{I}{r \times t}

Solving for the Annual Rate

When interest, principal, and time are known:

r=IP×tr = \frac{I}{P \times t}

To express the result as a percentage:

Rate (%)=r×100\text{Rate (\\%)} = r \times 100

Solving for Time

When interest, principal, and annual rate are known:

t=IP×rt = \frac{I}{P \times r}

Final Amount

After calculating interest:

A=P+IA = P + I

The final amount represents the original principal plus the calculated simple interest.

Worked Example

Suppose a principal of $10,000 earns simple interest at an annual rate of 5% for 3 years.

Inputs

  • Principal: $10,000
  • Annual rate: 5%
  • Time: 3 years

Step 1: Convert the Rate

5%÷100=0.055\\% \div 100 = 0.05

Step 2: Calculate Simple Interest

I=10,000×0.05×3I = 10,000 \times 0.05 \times 3 I=1,500I = 1,500

Step 3: Calculate the Final Amount

A=10,000+1,500A = 10,000 + 1,500 A=11,500A = 11,500

Final Result

Simple Interest: $1,500

Final Amount: $11,500

Understanding Your Results

Interest is the amount earned or charged over the specified period under the simple-interest formula.

Principal is the original amount before interest is added.

Annual Rate represents the yearly simple-interest rate. The percentage is converted to decimal form during the calculation.

Time shows how long the principal is subject to the stated annual rate, measured in years.

Final Amount is the principal plus the calculated interest.

These results are mathematical estimates based on the values entered and the simple-interest method. They should not be interpreted as a guaranteed loan cost, investment return, or financial offer.

Assumptions and Limitations

The calculator assumes that the simple-interest relationship I=PrtI = Prt applies for the entire period.

Interest is calculated from the stated principal rather than from previously accumulated interest, so this method does not model compound interest.

When solving backward for principal, rate, or time, the values used as divisors must not produce division by zero.

Actual financial products may calculate interest differently. For example, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains that some simple-interest auto loans calculate interest on the outstanding balance on a daily or monthly basis. Payment timing and changes in the outstanding balance can therefore affect the real amount of interest charged. (Consumer Financial Protection Bureau)

Always check the actual loan, savings, or investment agreement before using a basic simple-interest calculation for a financial decision.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the formula for simple interest?

The formula is:

I=P×r×tI = P \times r \times t

where PP is principal, rr is the annual rate expressed as a decimal, and tt is time in years. (OpenStax)

How do I convert an interest rate from a percentage to a decimal?

Divide the percentage by 100. For example, 6% becomes 0.06.

How do I calculate the total amount after simple interest?

First calculate the interest with I=PrtI = Prt, then add it to the principal:

A=P+IA = P + I
Can the calculator find the interest rate?

Yes. When interest, principal, and time are known, rearrange the formula:

r=IPtr = \frac{I}{Pt}

Then multiply the decimal result by 100 to express the annual rate as a percentage.

Can the calculator find how long an amount earns interest?

Yes. When interest, principal, and rate are known:

t=IPrt = \frac{I}{Pr}

The result is expressed in years.

Is simple interest the same as compound interest?

No. Simple interest uses the principal in the interest calculation, while compound interest can add previously accumulated interest to the balance used for later calculations. (OpenStax)

Will this calculator match the interest charged by my lender?

Not necessarily. A lender may use daily balances, monthly balances, amortization, fees, APR calculations, or other contract terms. CFPB guidance notes that real simple-interest loans may calculate interest using the outstanding balance rather than a fixed original principal for the entire term. (Consumer Financial Protection Bureau)
