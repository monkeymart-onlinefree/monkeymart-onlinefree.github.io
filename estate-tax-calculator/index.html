Use this Estate Tax Calculator to estimate a simplified 2026 U.S. federal estate tax. Enter estate assets, allowable deduction amounts, prior taxable gifts that used exclusion, and any available deceased spousal unused exclusion (DSUE) to estimate the amount potentially subject to the calculator's 40% tax rate.

For 2026, the IRS lists the federal basic exclusion amount and estate-tax filing threshold as $15,000,000 for a U.S. citizen or resident decedent.

What Is the Estate Tax Calculator?

The Estate Tax Calculator estimates how much of an estate may exceed the available federal estate-tax exclusion and calculates a simplified estimated tax on that excess.

It is designed for preliminary planning when you want to combine major estate assets, subtract entered debts and deductions, account for prior taxable gifts that used exclusion, and include available DSUE from a deceased spouse.

The result is an estimate only. Actual federal estate-tax liability is calculated under Form 706 rules, which use a unified rate schedule, applicable credits, prior taxable-gift calculations, and other adjustments.

Formula / Calculation Method

This calculator uses a simplified 2026 federal estate-tax model.

Main Formula

Estimated Estate Tax = 40% × max(0, Taxable Estate − Available Exclusion)

The taxable estate is calculated as:

Taxable Estate = Gross Estate − Total Entered Deductions

The available exclusion is calculated as:

Available Exclusion = $15,000,000 + DSUE − Prior Taxable Gifts Using Exclusion

The 2026 basic exclusion amount used by this calculator is $15,000,000.

Calculation Sequence

  1. Add all entered estate assets to calculate the gross estate.
  2. Add the entered debts and deduction amounts.
  3. Subtract total entered deductions from the gross estate to calculate the calculator's taxable estate.
  4. Start with the 2026 basic exclusion of $15,000,000, add available DSUE, and subtract prior taxable gifts that used exclusion.
  5. Subtract the available exclusion from the taxable estate and use zero if the result is negative.
  6. Multiply the remaining amount by 40% to calculate the simplified estimated estate tax.

The IRS Form 706 calculation is more detailed. Its unified rate schedule ranges from 18% to 40%, with the 40% rate applying to amounts above the top threshold in the rate schedule. The calculator intentionally uses the simpler 40% method described above rather than reproducing the full Form 706 computation.

Understanding Your Results

Gross Estate

Gross estate represents the total value of the asset amounts entered into the calculator before deductions.

Total Deductions

This is the combined amount entered for debts and mortgages, administration or funeral expenses, charitable deductions, and qualifying marital deductions.

Taxable Estate

The calculator subtracts total entered deductions from the gross estate to create the taxable-estate value used in its simplified calculation.

Available Exclusion

Available exclusion starts with the $15 million 2026 basic exclusion amount, adds entered DSUE, and subtracts prior taxable gifts entered as having used exclusion.

Amount Above the Exclusion

This is the portion of the calculator's taxable estate that remains after the available exclusion. If there is no positive excess, the simplified estimated tax is zero.

Estimated Estate Tax

The primary result is 40% of the amount above the calculated available exclusion.

A zero result does not automatically mean that no Form 706 filing requirement exists. For 2026, the IRS filing test generally considers the gross estate plus adjusted taxable gifts and certain other amounts, rather than simply the taxable estate after the deductions used by this calculator. A return may also be filed to elect DSUE portability regardless of estate size.

Assumptions and Limitations

This calculator is a simplified planning tool and does not reproduce the full Form 706 estate-tax computation.

It treats gross estate as the sum of the six entered asset categories and taxable estate as gross estate minus the four entered deduction categories.

The available exclusion is modeled as the 2026 $15 million basic exclusion plus entered DSUE minus prior taxable gifts that used exclusion.

Actual Form 706 calculations can treat prior taxable gifts through a more detailed unified tax and credit calculation. The IRS instructions state that estate tax generally applies unified rates to transfers during life and at death and then makes additional gift-tax and credit adjustments.

The calculator does not determine filing requirements, complex asset-inclusion rules, state estate tax, state inheritance tax, gift tax previously paid, special-use valuation, QTIP elections, trust taxation, or generation-skipping transfer tax.

It also cannot determine whether a particular debt, expense, marital transfer, charitable transfer, insurance policy, jointly owned property, trust interest, or other asset is legally deductible or includible in a specific estate.

State estate and inheritance taxes are outside this calculation and may apply separately.

Use the result for preliminary estimation rather than tax filing, legal advice, or estate-planning decisions requiring a complete review of the estate.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the federal estate-tax exemption for 2026?

The IRS lists the 2026 basic exclusion amount as $15,000,000. The IRS also lists $15,000,000 as the 2026 Form 706 filing threshold under its federal filing test for estates of U.S. citizens or residents.

Does this calculator use a 40% estate-tax rate?

Yes. The calculator multiplies the amount above its calculated available exclusion by 40%.

The official Form 706 calculation is more complex and uses a unified rate schedule ranging from 18% to 40%, together with applicable credits and prior-gift calculations.

What is DSUE?

DSUE means deceased spousal unused exclusion. When portability requirements are satisfied, unused exclusion from a deceased spouse may be transferred to the surviving spouse and added to the surviving spouse's applicable exclusion amount.

Are prior taxable gifts included?

Yes. This calculator asks for prior taxable gifts that used exclusion and subtracts that amount when determining the remaining available exclusion.

The actual federal estate-tax return uses more detailed calculations for adjusted taxable gifts, gift taxes, applicable credits, and previous transfers, so the calculator's treatment is a simplified estimate.

Does an estimated tax of $0 mean Form 706 does not have to be filed?

Not necessarily. The IRS filing test is different from this calculator's simplified taxable-estate formula. In addition, an estate may need to file Form 706 to elect portability of DSUE even when the estate is below the normal filing threshold.

Does the calculator include state estate or inheritance tax?

No. It estimates only the simplified U.S. federal estate tax described on this page. State estate and inheritance taxes are outside the calculation.

Can this calculator replace Form 706 or professional estate-tax advice?

No. Form 706 includes asset classification, valuation, deductions, prior gifts, credits, elections, portability, and other tax rules that this simplified calculator does not fully model. Complex estates should be reviewed using the applicable IRS rules and qualified professional advice.
