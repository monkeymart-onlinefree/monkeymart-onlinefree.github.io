The Payback Period Calculator estimates how long it takes for an investment to recover its initial cost. Enter the initial investment, annual net cash flow, and annual discount rate to calculate the simple payback period and evaluate discounted recovery time.

What Is the Payback Period Calculator?

A payback period measures the time required for cash generated by an investment to recover its initial cost. This calculator is useful for comparing projects, equipment purchases, business investments, or other decisions with an upfront cost and a steady annual net cash flow.

The simple payback method focuses on recovery time without adjusting for the time value of money. The discounted method reduces future cash flows to their present values before determining when the investment has been recovered.

How to Use the Payback Period Calculator

Input What to Enter
Initial Investment The total upfront amount invested in the project.
Annual Net Cash Flow The expected net cash generated each year after relevant operating cash inflows and outflows.
Discount Rate (%/yr) The annual percentage rate used to discount future cash flows for the discounted payback calculation.

Use the same currency for the initial investment and annual net cash flow. For example, if the initial investment is entered in dollars, the annual cash flow should also be entered in dollars.

Formula / Calculation Method

Main Formula

When annual net cash flow is the same each year, the simple payback period is:

Payback Period = Initial Investment ÷ Annual Net Cash Flow

Or:

PP = I ÷ C

OpenStax describes this calculation for projects with even annual net cash flows.

Variables

Symbol Meaning Unit
PP Simple payback period Years
I Initial investment Currency
C Annual net cash flow Currency per year
r Annual discount rate as a decimal Decimal per year
t Year number Years
DCFₜ Present value of the cash flow received in year t Currency

Calculation Sequence

The calculator first determines the simple payback period by dividing the initial investment by the annual net cash flow.

For discounted payback, the percentage discount rate is converted to a decimal:

r = Discount Rate (%) ÷ 100

Each future annual cash flow is then discounted:

DCFₜ = C ÷ (1 + r)ᵗ

The discounted cash flows are accumulated year by year until their cumulative present value reaches the initial investment. OpenStax defines discounted payback using this time-value-of-money approach.

Discounted Payback Period

If recovery occurs during a year rather than exactly at year-end, the fractional-year method can be expressed as:

Discounted Payback = Full Years Before Recovery + (Amount Still Unrecovered ÷ Discounted Cash Flow in Recovery Year)

This identifies the point during the recovery year when the discounted cash flows have covered the original investment.

Worked Example

Assume an investment has the following values:

Input Value
Initial investment $100,000
Annual net cash flow $25,000
Discount rate 8% per year

Step 1: Calculate the Simple Payback Period

PP = $100,000 ÷ $25,000
PP = 4 years

Without discounting future cash flows, the investment recovers its initial cost in four years.

Step 2: Discount the Annual Cash Flows

The annual discount rate is:

r = 8 ÷ 100 = 0.08

The first-year discounted cash flow is:

DCF₁ = $25,000 ÷ 1.08 = $23,148.15

Applying the same calculation each year gives:

Year Discounted Cash Flow Cumulative Discounted Cash Flow
1 $23,148.15 $23,148.15
2 $21,433.47 $44,581.62
3 $19,845.81 $64,427.42
4 $18,375.75 $82,803.17
5 $17,014.58 $99,817.75
6 $15,754.24 $115,571.99

After five years, $182.25 of the original investment remains unrecovered on a discounted basis.

Step 3: Calculate the Fraction of the Recovery Year

Fraction = $182.25 ÷ $15,754.24
Fraction ≈ 0.0116 years

Final Result

Simple payback period: 4 years

Discounted payback period: approximately 5.01 years, with discounted recovery occurring during year 6.

The discounted period is longer because future cash flows are worth less when converted to present value.

Understanding Your Results

Simple Payback Period shows how many years of equal annual net cash flow are required to recover the original investment without discounting future money.

Discounted Payback Period measures recovery using the present value of future cash flows. A positive discount rate generally makes the discounted recovery time longer than the simple payback time because later cash flows receive a lower present value.

The result is a recovery-time measure rather than a complete measure of profitability. Two investments can have similar payback periods but produce very different total cash flows after the initial investment has been recovered.

Assumptions and Limitations

The simple formula assumes the annual net cash flow remains constant from year to year. If actual cash flows vary substantially, dividing the initial investment by one annual cash-flow figure may not represent the true recovery schedule.

Simple payback does not account for the time value of money. Discounted payback addresses that issue by discounting future cash flows, but it still focuses only on how long recovery takes. Both methods can ignore important cash flows occurring after the payback point.

The calculator does not by itself measure total profitability, net present value, internal rate of return, investment risk, financing costs, taxes, or strategic benefits. Payback results are therefore most useful as one part of a broader investment analysis.

Results also depend directly on the cash-flow and discount-rate assumptions entered. Actual business cash flows can differ from projections.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the formula for the payback period?

For equal annual net cash flows:

Payback Period = Initial Investment ÷ Annual Net Cash Flow

For example, a $60,000 investment generating $15,000 per year has a simple payback period of four years.

What is the difference between simple and discounted payback?

Simple payback uses the future cash flows at their stated amounts. Discounted payback first converts future cash flows to present value using the entered discount rate, then determines when those discounted amounts recover the investment.

Why is the discounted payback period usually longer?

With a positive discount rate, a future cash flow has a lower present value than the same amount received today. Therefore, more time may be required for discounted cash flows to recover the original investment.

What happens if I enter a 0% discount rate?

At a 0% discount rate, future annual cash flows are not reduced for time value. With equal annual cash flows, the discounted calculation therefore matches the simple payback calculation.

Can an investment fail to reach discounted payback?

Yes. If the present value of future cash flows never becomes large enough to cover the initial investment, there is no discounted payback within that cash-flow pattern. A high discount rate or relatively small annual cash flow can produce this situation.

Does a shorter payback period mean an investment is more profitable?

Not necessarily. Payback measures recovery time, not total profit. It ignores cash flows occurring after the recovery point, so an investment with a longer payback could still generate more value over its full life.

Should payback period be used by itself for an investment decision?

Payback can provide useful information about recovery time and liquidity, but it does not capture every financial factor. NPV, IRR, ROI, risk, and expected cash flows over the full project life may provide additional information for evaluating an investment.
