The BTU Calculator estimates the cooling capacity a room air conditioner may need in BTU per hour (BTU/h). Enter the room area, sun exposure, number of people regularly using the room, and room use to get an adjusted cooling-capacity estimate based on room-size guidance and common room adjustments.

What Is a BTU Calculator?

A BTU Calculator helps estimate how much cooling capacity is suitable for a room. This calculator starts with the room's floor area, matches it to a base room-air-conditioner capacity, and then adjusts that capacity for sunlight, occupancy, and kitchen use.

The result is expressed in BTU/h, which measures cooling capacity over time. A BTU itself is a unit of heat energy; room air conditioners are rated in BTUs per hour.

This tool is useful when comparing room or window air conditioners. It is not intended to replace a professional whole-home HVAC load calculation.

How to Use the BTU Calculator

1. Enter Room Area

Enter the floor area of the room in square feet (ft²).

If you still need to calculate the room area, a rectangular room can be measured as:

Room Area = Length × Width

ENERGY STAR recommends measuring room dimensions in feet when determining the square footage to be cooled.

2. Select Sun Exposure

Choose the option that best describes the room:

  • Normal exposure: no sunlight adjustment.
  • Heavy shade: cooling capacity is reduced by 10%.
  • Very sunny: cooling capacity is increased by 10%.

3. Enter People Regularly in the Room

Enter the number of people who regularly occupy the space.

The base recommendation covers up to two people. For each person above two, the calculator adds 600 BTU/h.

4. Select Room Use

Choose whether the space is a normal room or a kitchen.

For kitchen use, the calculator adds 4,000 BTU/h to account for the additional heat commonly produced in the room.

Formula / Calculation Method

This calculator does not use one universal "BTU per square foot" equation. It uses a room-area lookup table followed by conditional adjustments.

Step 2: Adjust for Sun Exposure

Let:

  • B = base cooling capacity in BTU/h

For a heavily shaded room:

Shade-adjusted capacity = B × 0.90

For a very sunny room:

Sunny-adjusted capacity = B × 1.10

For normal sun exposure, no percentage adjustment is applied.

ENERGY STAR currently recommends reducing capacity by 10% for heavy shade and increasing it by 10% for a very sunny room.

Step 3: Adjust for Additional People

Let:

  • P = number of people regularly occupying the room

When more than two people regularly use the room:

Occupancy Addition = max(P − 2, 0) × 600 BTU/h

For example, four regular occupants create two additional-person adjustments:

(4 − 2) × 600 = 1,200 BTU/h

Step 4: Adjust for Kitchen Use

If the room is a kitchen:

Kitchen Addition = 4,000 BTU/h

For a normal room:

Kitchen Addition = 0 BTU/h

Calculation Sequence

The calculator:

  1. Reads the room area in square feet.
  2. Finds the matching base BTU/h capacity.
  3. Applies the selected sun-exposure adjustment.
  4. Adds capacity for occupants above two, when applicable.
  5. Adds the kitchen adjustment when the room is used as a kitchen.
  6. Returns the adjusted cooling-capacity recommendation in BTU/h.

Worked Example

Suppose a room has the following conditions.

Inputs

  • Room area: 375 ft²
  • Sun exposure: Very sunny
  • People regularly in room: 2
  • Room use: Normal room

Step 1: Find the Base Capacity

A 375 ft² room falls within the 350 up to 400 ft² range.

Base capacity:

B = 9,000 BTU/h

Step 2: Apply the Sun Adjustment

Because the room is very sunny:

9,000 × 10% = 900 BTU/h

Add this adjustment to the base capacity:

9,000 + 900 = 9,900 BTU/h

Step 3: Check Occupancy

There are two people, so no additional occupancy capacity is added.

Occupancy Addition = 0 BTU/h

Step 4: Check Room Use

The room is not a kitchen.

Kitchen Addition = 0 BTU/h

Final Result

Estimated cooling capacity = 9,900 BTU/h

The result is a sizing estimate based on the room-area table and selected room conditions. Actual air conditioners are sold in specific capacity sizes, so available equipment may not exactly match the calculated number.

Understanding Your Results

Estimated Cooling Capacity

The main result is the recommended cooling capacity in BTU per hour.

A higher BTU/h result means the room conditions indicate a greater cooling-capacity requirement. Room size is the starting point, while sunlight, occupancy, and kitchen use can move the recommendation higher or lower.

ENERGY STAR notes that simply buying a larger air conditioner is not necessarily better. An oversized unit can cool the space quickly without removing enough humidity, which may reduce comfort and efficiency.

BTU vs. BTU/h

A BTU is a unit of heat energy. The U.S. Energy Information Administration describes one Btu as approximately the heat required to raise one pound of water by 1°F.

For an air conditioner, BTU/h describes how much cooling capacity the equipment provides over an hour.

Current Guidance Used by This Calculator

Checked September 24, 2026, ENERGY STAR's room-air-conditioner guidance continues to use room-size capacity bands from 5,000 BTU/h for 100–150 ft² through 34,000 BTU/h for 2,000–2,500 ft². Its published adjustments also remain:

  • Heavy shade: −10%
  • Very sunny room: +10%
  • More than two regular occupants: +600 BTU/h per additional person
  • Kitchen: +4,000 BTU/h

The published sizing capacities are based on an 8-foot ceiling.

Assumptions and Limitations

This calculator provides a room air-conditioner sizing estimate, not a complete HVAC design calculation.

The underlying room-size guidance assumes an 8-foot ceiling. Higher ceilings may require more cooling capacity than the area table alone suggests.

Actual cooling loads can also change because of factors that this calculator does not fully model, including:

  • Building insulation and envelope quality
  • Number, size, orientation, and efficiency of windows
  • Outdoor climate and design temperature
  • Air leakage and ventilation
  • Ceiling height
  • Heat-producing equipment
  • Building construction
  • Humidity and latent cooling load
  • Equipment efficiency
  • Duct losses
  • Whole-home HVAC system design

The ENERGY STAR sizing table shown here covers spaces from 100 up to 2,500 ft². For spaces outside that published range, the table itself does not provide a standard capacity band.

For central air conditioning, heat pumps, unusual rooms, high ceilings, large glass areas, or whole-home equipment sizing, a professional HVAC load calculation may be more appropriate.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many BTUs do I need for my room?

Enter the room area into the calculator. It first matches the area to a base BTU/h capacity and then adjusts that recommendation for sunlight, occupancy, and kitchen use.

Is BTU the same as BTU per hour?

No. BTU is a unit of heat energy, while BTU/h is a rate of heating or cooling capacity. Room air conditioners are normally sized by cooling capacity in BTUs per hour.

Does a sunny room need more BTUs?

Yes. The method used by this calculator increases the base cooling capacity by 10% when the room is classified as very sunny.

Does a shaded room need fewer BTUs?

Under the sizing guidance used here, a heavily shaded room receives a 10% reduction from the base cooling capacity.

How do extra people affect the BTU calculation?

If more than two people regularly occupy the room, the calculation adds 600 BTU/h for each additional person. Four regular occupants, for example, add 1,200 BTU/h.

Why does a kitchen need more cooling capacity?

Kitchens can contain significant additional heat sources. The calculation therefore adds 4,000 BTU/h when the selected room use is a kitchen.

Can I use this calculator to size central air conditioning?

It is intended for a room-level air-conditioner estimate. Central HVAC sizing depends on more detailed building, climate, ventilation, envelope, and system information, so a professional load calculation may be needed.
