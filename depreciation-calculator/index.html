Use this Depreciation Calculator to build a full-year depreciation schedule using the straight-line, declining-balance, or sum-of-years-digits method. Enter the asset cost, salvage value, useful life, and—when using declining balance—the depreciation factor to calculate annual depreciation and book value.

What Is the Depreciation Calculator?

The Depreciation Calculator estimates how an asset's depreciable cost is allocated over its useful life.

It supports three methods:

  • Straight-line depreciation spreads depreciation evenly across the asset's useful life.
  • Declining-balance depreciation applies a fixed rate to the asset's beginning book value, producing larger depreciation amounts in earlier years.
  • Sum-of-years-digits (SYD) applies a decreasing fraction to the depreciable base, creating another accelerated depreciation schedule.

The calculator is useful for comparing depreciation methods and reviewing annual depreciation expense, accumulated depreciation, and remaining book value.

How to Use the Depreciation Calculator

  1. Method: Select straight-line, declining balance, or sum-of-years-digits.
  2. Declining-balance factor: Enter the factor used to calculate the declining-balance rate. This input applies to the declining-balance method.
  3. Asset cost: Enter the original cost of the asset.
  4. Salvage value: Enter the estimated value remaining at the end of the asset's useful life.
  5. Useful life: Enter the number of years over which the asset will be depreciated.

For a conventional depreciation schedule, useful life must be greater than zero and salvage value normally should not exceed asset cost.

The depreciable base is:

Depreciable Base = C − S

Straight-Line Depreciation

Straight-line depreciation allocates the depreciable base equally across the useful life.

Annual Depreciation = (C − S) ÷ n

Therefore:

Dₜ = (C − S) / n

The same depreciation amount is applied for each full year.

This standard straight-line relationship is also described by the IRS and accounting references.

Declining-Balance Depreciation

The calculator derives the annual declining-balance rate from the selected factor and useful life:

r = f ÷ n

Annual depreciation is then based on the book value at the beginning of the year:

Dₜ = Beginning Book Value × r

The depreciation amount is limited so that the asset's book value does not fall below the entered salvage value:

Dₜ = min(Bₜ₋₁ × r, Bₜ₋₁ − S)

The ending book value is:

Bₜ = Bₜ₋₁ − Dₜ

For example, a factor of 2 produces a double-declining rate. A five-year useful life with a factor of 2 gives:

2 ÷ 5 = 0.40 = 40% per year

Applying the rate to beginning book value and stopping at salvage value is consistent with standard declining-balance methodology.

Sum-of-Years-Digits Depreciation

First calculate the sum of the years' digits:

SYD = n(n + 1) ÷ 2

Annual depreciation is:

Dₜ = (C − S) × (n − t + 1) ÷ [n(n + 1) ÷ 2]

For a five-year asset:

SYD = 5 × 6 ÷ 2 = 15

The yearly fractions are therefore:

  • Year 1: 5/15
  • Year 2: 4/15
  • Year 3: 3/15
  • Year 4: 2/15
  • Year 5: 1/15

This makes depreciation larger in earlier years and smaller in later years.

Accumulated Depreciation

After year t:

Accumulated Depreciation = D₁ + D₂ + ... + Dₜ

Book Value

The asset's book value after depreciation is:

Book Value = Asset Cost − Accumulated Depreciation

Under this calculator's model, depreciation does not reduce the ending book value below the entered salvage value.

Worked Example

Assume an asset has:

Inputs

  • Asset cost: $50,000
  • Salvage value: $5,000
  • Useful life: 5 years
  • Declining-balance factor: 2

The depreciable base is:

$50,000 − $5,000 = $45,000

Step 1: Straight-Line Method

Annual depreciation = $45,000 ÷ 5 = $9,000

Each year receives $9,000 of depreciation.

Step 2: Declining-Balance Method

First calculate the rate:

2 ÷ 5 = 40%

Year 1:

$50,000 × 40% = $20,000

Ending book value:

$50,000 − $20,000 = $30,000

Year 2:

$30,000 × 40% = $12,000

The same process continues using each year's beginning book value. In the final year, depreciation is limited to the amount required to reach the $5,000 salvage value.

Step 3: Sum-of-Years-Digits Method

The sum of the years is:

5 × (5 + 1) ÷ 2 = 15

Year 1 depreciation:

$45,000 × 5/15 = $15,000

Year 2 depreciation:

$45,000 × 4/15 = $12,000

The fraction continues decreasing until the fifth year.

Final Result

All three methods depreciate the same $45,000 depreciable base in this example. The difference is when depreciation is recognized: straight-line spreads it evenly, while declining balance and SYD recognize more depreciation in earlier years.

Understanding Your Results

Annual Depreciation

Annual depreciation is the amount allocated to depreciation for a specific year.

Straight-line normally produces the same annual amount, while declining-balance and SYD generally produce larger amounts in the first years and smaller amounts later.

Accumulated Depreciation

Accumulated depreciation is the total depreciation calculated from the first year through the selected year.

For example:

Accumulated Depreciation after Year 3 = Year 1 + Year 2 + Year 3 depreciation

Book Value

Book value is the portion of the asset's original cost remaining after accumulated depreciation:

Book Value = Cost − Accumulated Depreciation

Book value in this calculator is an accounting calculation. It is not necessarily the asset's current resale or market value.

Salvage Value

Salvage value, also called residual or scrap value, is the estimated value expected to remain at the end of the useful life. It reduces the amount depreciated under the straight-line and SYD calculations and acts as the minimum book-value floor in the declining-balance calculation.

Assumptions and Limitations

This calculator uses a simplified full-year depreciation model.

It assumes:

  • A fixed asset cost.
  • A fixed salvage value.
  • A useful life stated in years.
  • Full-year depreciation periods.
  • The selected depreciation method remains in use throughout the schedule.

The calculator does not apply:

  • Half-year conventions.
  • Mid-quarter conventions.
  • Mid-month conventions.
  • Partial-year depreciation.
  • MACRS depreciation tables.
  • Automatic switching from declining balance to straight-line.
  • Asset impairment.
  • Revaluation.
  • Component depreciation.
  • Asset-disposal rules.

For U.S. tax purposes, depreciation can involve recovery classes, conventions, statutory rates, eligibility requirements, and method-switching rules. IRS Publication 946 (2025) describes those rules, so this general calculator should not be treated as a MACRS or tax-return calculator.

Actual accounting or tax depreciation can therefore differ from this calculator's estimates.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between straight-line and declining-balance depreciation?

Straight-line depreciation divides the depreciable base evenly over the useful life. Declining balance applies a rate to the remaining book value, so depreciation is usually higher in earlier years.

What factor should I use for double-declining balance?

Use a declining-balance factor of 2.

For example, with a five-year useful life:

Rate = 2 ÷ 5 = 40%

A factor of 2 represents twice the straight-line rate.

What is salvage value?

Salvage value is the estimated amount the asset will be worth at the end of its useful life. For straight-line and SYD depreciation:

Depreciable Base = Asset Cost − Salvage Value
Can declining-balance depreciation reduce book value below salvage value?

No. This calculator limits depreciation when necessary so the book value does not fall below the entered salvage value.

Is this calculator the same as MACRS depreciation?

No. MACRS is a U.S. federal tax depreciation system with prescribed recovery periods, depreciation methods, conventions, and other rules. This calculator uses general straight-line, declining-balance, and SYD methods rather than MACRS tables.

Does the calculator support partial-year depreciation?

No. The model assumes full-year depreciation. It does not apply half-year, mid-quarter, mid-month, or other partial-year conventions.

Why do accelerated methods show more depreciation in early years?

Declining-balance and SYD methods are designed to allocate a greater portion of depreciation to earlier periods. Straight-line depreciation instead allocates the depreciable base evenly across the useful life.
