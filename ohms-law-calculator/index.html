Use the Ohm’s Law Calculator to find voltage, current, resistance, or electrical power from any two known values. Enter the values in supported electrical units, and the calculator converts them to base units before solving for the remaining quantities.

What Is the Ohm’s Law Calculator?

The Ohm’s Law Calculator solves the relationship between voltage, current, resistance, and electrical power in a resistive circuit.

It is useful when you know two electrical values and need to calculate the other two. For example, if voltage and resistance are known, the calculator can determine current and power.

How to Use the Ohm’s Law Calculator

Enter any two known electrical values.

Voltage

Enter the electrical potential difference.

Supported units include:

  • Millivolts (mV)
  • Volts (V)
  • Kilovolts (kV)

Current

Enter the electrical current.

Supported units include:

Formula / Calculation Method

Main Formula

Ohm’s law relates voltage, current, and resistance:

V = I × R

It can also be rearranged as:

I = V ÷ R
R = V ÷ I

For an ohmic device, voltage is proportional to current, with resistance providing the proportional relationship.

Electrical power is calculated as:

P = V × I

Combining the power equation with Ohm’s law gives:

P = I² × R

and:

P = V² ÷ R

These relationships are standard power equations for resistive loads.

Variables

Symbol Meaning Base Unit
V Voltage volt (V)
I Current ampere (A)
R Resistance ohm (Ω)
P Electrical power watt (W)

NIST defines the volt as the SI unit of electric potential difference and the ohm as the SI unit of electrical resistance, with 1 Ω = 1 V/A.

Unit Conversion

Before solving, supported prefixed units are converted to base units:

  • 1 mV = 0.001 V
  • 1 kV = 1,000 V
  • 1 mA = 0.001 A
  • 1 kΩ = 1,000 Ω
  • 1 MΩ = 1,000,000 Ω
  • 1 mW = 0.001 W
  • 1 kW = 1,000 W

These conversions follow standard SI prefix factors.

Calculation Sequence

  1. Read the two known electrical values.
  2. Convert their selected units to base units.
  3. Identify the appropriate Ohm’s law or power relationship.
  4. Calculate the first unknown quantity.
  5. Use the resulting value to calculate the remaining unknown quantity.
  6. Present voltage, current, resistance, and power.

Calculating from Voltage and Current

If V and I are known:

R = V ÷ I
P = V × I

Calculating from Voltage and Resistance

If V and R are known:

I = V ÷ R
P = V² ÷ R

Calculating from Current and Resistance

If I and R are known:

V = I × R
P = I² × R

Calculating from Voltage and Power

For positive-magnitude values:

I = P ÷ V
R = V² ÷ P

Calculating from Current and Power

For positive-magnitude values:

V = P ÷ I
R = P ÷ I²

Calculating from Resistance and Power

For positive-magnitude values:

I = √(P ÷ R)
V = √(P × R)

These forms are algebraic rearrangements of V = IR and P = VI.

Worked Example

Suppose a circuit has:

Inputs

  • Voltage = 12 V
  • Resistance = 6 kΩ

We want to find current and electrical power.

Step 1: Convert Resistance

Convert kilo-ohms to ohms:

6 kΩ = 6 × 1,000 = 6,000 Ω

Step 2: Calculate Current

Use:

I = V ÷ R

Substitute the values:

I = 12 ÷ 6,000
I = 0.002 A

That is equivalent to:

I = 2 mA

Step 3: Calculate Power

Use:

P = V × I
P = 12 × 0.002
P = 0.024 W

That is equivalent to:

P = 24 mW

Final Result

For a 12 V supply across 6 kΩ:

  • Voltage = 12 V
  • Resistance = 6 kΩ
  • Current = 2 mA
  • Power = 24 mW

Understanding Your Results

Voltage

Voltage represents the electrical potential difference across the component or circuit. The base SI unit is the volt (V).

Current

Current represents the rate of electric charge flow. It is measured in amperes (A).

Resistance

Resistance describes how strongly a component opposes electric current. It is measured in ohms (Ω).

Power

Power represents the rate at which electrical energy is transferred or dissipated. It is measured in watts (W).

For a resistive component, increasing current can increase power rapidly because the relationship P = I²R contains the square of the current.

Assumptions and Limitations

This calculator uses the standard mathematical relationships between voltage, current, resistance, and power. Those equations work directly for resistive or approximately ohmic behavior.

Real electrical components may behave differently because resistance can change with temperature, operating conditions, manufacturing tolerances, self-heating, or nonlinear behavior.

Circuits containing capacitors or inductors may require impedance and phase-angle calculations instead of resistance alone. A basic resistance calculation therefore does not provide a complete AC circuit analysis.

The result is also only as reliable as the entered values. Measurement uncertainty and actual component tolerance are not automatically included.

Some zero-value combinations cannot determine every unknown quantity. For example, if both voltage and current are zero, dividing voltage by current cannot establish a unique resistance.

Use component datasheets and appropriate electrical design practices when checking maximum voltage, current, power dissipation, temperature, or safety limits.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I calculate Ohm’s law with only two known values?

Yes. The calculator is designed to solve voltage, current, resistance, and power from any two known quantities when the combination provides enough information to determine the remaining values.

What is the basic Ohm’s law formula?

The basic equation is:

V = I × R

where V is voltage, I is current, and R is resistance.

How is electrical power related to Ohm’s law?

The basic electrical power equation is:

P = V × I

For an ohmic resistor, combining this equation with Ohm’s law also gives P = I²R and P = V²/R.

Can I enter millivolts, kilo-ohms, or milliwatts?

Yes. The calculator supports millivolts, volts, kilovolts, milliamps, amps, ohms, kilo-ohms, mega-ohms, milliwatts, watts, and kilowatts. Prefixed values are converted to base units before the formulas are applied.

Does Ohm’s law work for every electrical component?

No. Ohm’s law directly describes ohmic behavior where the relationship between voltage and current can be represented by a resistance. Components such as diodes, transistors, thermistors, and other nonlinear devices may not maintain a constant resistance across operating conditions.

Can I use this calculator for AC circuits?

It can describe a purely resistive relationship, but AC circuits containing significant capacitance or inductance may require impedance, reactance, phase angle, power factor, and related calculations. The simple resistance formulas should not be treated as a complete AC analysis.

Why might a measured value differ from the calculator result?

Real resistors and circuits have tolerances, temperature effects, contact and lead resistance, measurement uncertainty, and other physical effects. The calculator evaluates the mathematical values entered; it does not model all real-world variation.
