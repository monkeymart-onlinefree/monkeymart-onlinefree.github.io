The Marriage Tax Calculator compares the estimated 2026 U.S. federal ordinary income tax for two people before marriage with the estimated tax if they file married filing jointly. Enter each person's annual ordinary income, pre-tax deductions, pre-marriage filing status, and deduction information to estimate whether the simplified comparison produces tax savings or a marriage penalty.

What Is the Marriage Tax Calculator?

The Marriage Tax Calculator is a planning tool for comparing two simplified federal income-tax scenarios for tax year 2026:

  • Each person's estimated federal ordinary income tax before marriage.
  • The estimated federal ordinary income tax on their combined income when filing married filing jointly.

The difference shows whether the joint estimate is higher, lower, or equal to the two pre-marriage estimates combined.

This calculator focuses on ordinary federal income tax. It is not a complete federal tax return calculator.

How to Use the Marriage Tax Calculator

For each person, enter the applicable information requested by the calculator.

Annual ordinary income: Enter annual income that will be treated as ordinary income in this simplified calculation.

Pre-tax deductions: Enter deductions that reduce the income used by the calculator before taxable income is calculated.

Before-marriage status: Select the applicable filing status used to estimate that person's tax before marriage. Filing status affects the applicable standard deduction and progressive federal tax brackets.

Itemized deduction: Enter the applicable itemized deduction amount. Entering 0 tells the calculator to use the applicable 2026 standard deduction instead.

Joint itemized deduction: Enter the deduction to use for the married filing jointly calculation. Enter 0 to use the 2026 married filing jointly standard deduction.

After entering the information for both people, calculate the result to compare the combined pre-marriage estimate with the married filing jointly estimate.

Formula / Calculation Method

Main Formula

The calculator uses:

Marriage Difference = Joint Federal Tax − (Spouse 1 Pre-Marriage Tax + Spouse 2 Pre-Marriage Tax)

Where:

  • Marriage Difference > 0: estimated marriage tax penalty under this simplified model.
  • Marriage Difference < 0: estimated marriage tax savings.
  • Marriage Difference = 0: no estimated federal ordinary-income tax difference.

Variables

I₁ = Spouse 1 annual ordinary income, in dollars
I₂ = Spouse 2 annual ordinary income, in dollars
P₁ = Spouse 1 pre-tax deductions, in dollars
P₂ = Spouse 2 pre-tax deductions, in dollars
D₁ = deduction used for Spouse 1
D₂ = deduction used for Spouse 2
Dⱼ = deduction used for the joint calculation
T₁ = estimated pre-marriage federal tax for Spouse 1
T₂ = estimated pre-marriage federal tax for Spouse 2
Tⱼ = estimated married filing jointly federal tax
Δ = marriage difference

Taxable Income

For each pre-marriage calculation:

Taxable Income₁ = max(0, I₁ − P₁ − D₁)
Taxable Income₂ = max(0, I₂ − P₂ − D₂)

For the married filing jointly calculation:

Joint Taxable Income = max(0, I₁ + I₂ − P₁ − P₂ − Dⱼ)

When an itemized-deduction input is 0, the calculator uses the applicable standard deduction. A non-zero itemized-deduction input is used for that scenario.

Progressive Tax Calculation

The calculator does not multiply all taxable income by one tax rate. It applies the 2026 federal ordinary-income brackets progressively.

For each bracket:

Tax for Bracket = Income Falling Within the Bracket × Marginal Tax Rate

The taxes from all applicable brackets are then added together.

The IRS's 2026 schedules use ordinary-income marginal rates from 10% through 37%, with different taxable-income thresholds depending on filing status.

Calculation Sequence

  1. Read each person's annual ordinary income.
  2. Subtract the entered pre-tax deductions.
  3. Determine the deduction used for each pre-marriage scenario.
  4. Calculate each person's taxable income.
  5. Apply the applicable 2026 progressive federal tax schedule to each person's taxable income.
  6. Add the two pre-marriage tax estimates.
  7. Combine both incomes and pre-tax deductions.
  8. Apply the joint deduction.
  9. Calculate joint taxable income.
  10. Apply the 2026 married filing jointly progressive tax brackets.
  11. Subtract the combined pre-marriage tax from the joint tax estimate.

Worked Example

Assume both people would use the Single filing status before marriage and use standard deductions.

Inputs

Spouse 1

  • Annual ordinary income: $95,000
  • Pre-tax deductions: $0
  • Itemized deduction: $0

Spouse 2

  • Annual ordinary income: $60,000
  • Pre-tax deductions: $0
  • Itemized deduction: $0

Joint itemized deduction: $0

For tax year 2026, the IRS standard deduction is $16,100 for Single filers and $32,200 for Married Filing Jointly.

Step 1: Calculate Pre-Marriage Taxable Income

Spouse 1:

$95,000 − $16,100 = $78,900

Spouse 2:

$60,000 − $16,100 = $43,900

Step 2: Calculate Pre-Marriage Federal Tax

For a Single filer in 2026, the first $12,400 of taxable income is taxed at 10%, the portion from $12,400 to $50,400 at 12%, and the next applicable portion at 22%.

Spouse 1:

  • $12,400 × 10% = $1,240
  • $38,000 × 12% = $4,560
  • $28,500 × 22% = $6,270
Estimated Spouse 1 tax = $12,070

Spouse 2:

  • $12,400 × 10% = $1,240
  • $31,500 × 12% = $3,780
Estimated Spouse 2 tax = $5,020

Combined pre-marriage estimate:

$12,070 + $5,020 = $17,090

Step 3: Calculate Joint Taxable Income

Combined ordinary income:

$95,000 + $60,000 = $155,000

Subtract the 2026 married filing jointly standard deduction:

$155,000 − $32,200 = $122,800

Step 4: Calculate Married Filing Jointly Tax

For Married Filing Jointly in 2026, the first $24,800 is taxed at 10%, the portion from $24,800 to $100,800 at 12%, and the next applicable portion at 22%.

  • $24,800 × 10% = $2,480
  • $76,000 × 12% = $9,120
  • $22,000 × 22% = $4,840
Estimated joint federal tax = $16,440

Final Result

Marriage Difference = $16,440 − $17,090
Marriage Difference = −$650

Under this simplified calculation, the negative result represents approximately $650 in estimated federal ordinary-income tax savings from the married filing jointly scenario.

Understanding Your Results

Marriage Difference

This is the main comparison result.

A positive amount means the estimated married filing jointly tax is higher than the two pre-marriage estimates combined.

A negative amount means the joint estimate is lower.

Pre-Marriage Tax

Each person's pre-marriage tax is calculated independently using that person's income, pre-tax deductions, deduction choice, filing status, and applicable 2026 ordinary-income brackets.

Combined Pre-Marriage Tax

This is:

Spouse 1 Estimated Tax + Spouse 2 Estimated Tax

It provides the baseline used for comparison.

Joint Federal Tax

This is the estimated ordinary federal income tax after combining both people's applicable income and deductions and applying the married filing jointly tax schedule.

These figures are planning estimates. They are not guaranteed tax liabilities or refunds.

Current 2026 Tax Rules Used

For tax year 2026, the standard deductions relevant to federal individual income-tax calculations include:

  • Single or Married Filing Separately: $16,100
  • Married Filing Jointly or Qualifying Surviving Spouse: $32,200
  • Head of Household: $24,150

The 2026 ordinary federal income-tax schedules use marginal rates of 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35%, and 37%. The married filing jointly brackets differ from the brackets used for individual filing statuses.

These figures apply to tax year 2026 and should be reviewed when federal tax parameters change.

Assumptions and Limitations

This calculator is a simplified federal ordinary-income tax comparison.

It does not calculate:

  • Child Tax Credit
  • Earned Income Tax Credit
  • Education credits
  • Qualified-dividend tax treatment
  • Long-term or short-term capital-gain tax rules
  • Self-employment tax
  • Social Security or Medicare payroll taxes
  • State or local income taxes
  • Alternative Minimum Tax
  • Net Investment Income Tax
  • Income-based credit or deduction phaseouts
  • Other special federal tax provisions

The calculator compares the pre-marriage estimates with Married Filing Jointly. It is not a Married Filing Jointly versus Married Filing Separately optimizer.

Actual itemized deductions may also be subject to eligibility rules, limitations, or adjustments that are not modeled by this simplified comparison.

No special tax-return rounding method is represented here. Small differences may therefore exist between this estimate and calculations produced using official tax forms, tax software, or a tax professional.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does a positive marriage tax result mean?

A positive marriage difference means the estimated married filing jointly federal ordinary-income tax is higher than the two pre-marriage tax estimates combined. Under this calculator's limited model, that difference is an estimated marriage penalty.

What does a negative result mean?

A negative result means the estimated married filing jointly tax is lower than the combined pre-marriage estimates. The absolute value of the result represents estimated tax savings under the simplified calculation.

Does this calculator use 2026 federal tax brackets?

Yes. The calculation is based on the IRS ordinary-income brackets and standard deductions for tax year 2026.

What happens if I enter 0 for itemized deductions?

An itemized-deduction value of 0 tells the calculator to use the applicable standard deduction for that calculation. For the joint calculation, 0 uses the 2026 married filing jointly standard deduction.

Does the calculator include tax credits?

No. Credits such as the Child Tax Credit, Earned Income Tax Credit, and education credits are outside this simplified calculation. Because credits can materially change actual tax liability, real tax results may differ.

Does this compare Married Filing Jointly with Married Filing Separately?

No. The calculator compares two pre-marriage tax estimates with a married filing jointly estimate. It does not calculate whether Married Filing Separately would produce a different result.

Does the date of marriage affect federal filing status?

Filing status generally depends on marital status on the last day of the tax year. If taxpayers are considered married at year-end, married filing statuses generally apply for that tax year, subject to IRS rules and exceptions.
