The RMD Calculator estimates your required minimum distribution using your age at year-end, prior December 31 retirement-account balance, and the applicable IRS distribution period. It also accepts an estimated annual return for the calculator’s 5-year projection.

What Is the RMD Calculator?

A required minimum distribution, or RMD, is the minimum amount that certain retirement-account owners must withdraw for a year.

This calculator estimates the current-year RMD by dividing the previous year-end account balance by the applicable IRS distribution period. Most account owners use the Uniform Lifetime Table, while a different factor can apply when a spouse is the sole beneficiary and is more than 10 years younger.

The calculator is useful for retirement withdrawal planning, estimating an upcoming distribution, and understanding how the applicable IRS factor affects the required amount.

How to Use the RMD Calculator

Enter the following information in the calculator:

  1. Age at year-end: Enter your age as of your birthday in the RMD year. The IRS distribution period is based on your age for that year.
  2. Prior Dec. 31 account balance: Enter the retirement account balance at the end of the previous calendar year. For a 2026 RMD, this is generally the account balance at the end of December 31, 2025.
  3. Spouse more than 10 years younger and sole beneficiary?: Select the applicable answer. This determines whether the standard Uniform Lifetime factor or a Joint Life factor may apply.
  4. IRS Joint Life factor: If your spouse is your sole beneficiary and is more than 10 years younger, enter the applicable factor from the IRS Joint and Last Survivor Life Expectancy Table.
  5. Estimated annual return for 5-year projection (%): Enter the annual investment-return assumption you want the calculator to use for its projection. This is an estimate and does not guarantee future investment performance.

Formula / Calculation Method

Main Formula

The calculator uses:

RMD = B ÷ D

where:

  • RMD = estimated required minimum distribution in dollars
  • B = prior December 31 account balance in dollars
  • D = applicable IRS distribution-period factor

The IRS uses the previous year-end account balance and the applicable distribution period to determine a standard annual RMD.

Calculation Sequence

  1. Determine your age at the end of the distribution year.
  2. Identify which IRS life-expectancy table applies.
  3. Use the Uniform Lifetime factor for the standard owner calculation.
  4. If your spouse is your sole beneficiary and is more than 10 years younger, use the applicable Joint Life factor instead.
  5. Divide the prior December 31 account balance by the applicable factor.
  6. Display the estimated RMD.

The estimated annual return does not change this current-year RMD formula. It is an assumption used for the calculator’s 5-year projection.

Uniform Lifetime Method

For most retirement-account owners calculating their own RMD:

RMD = Prior Dec. 31 balance ÷ Uniform Lifetime factor

The Uniform Lifetime Table generally applies when you are unmarried, when your spouse is not more than 10 years younger, or when your spouse is not your sole beneficiary.

Younger-Spouse Method

If your spouse is your sole beneficiary and is more than 10 years younger, the applicable Joint and Last Survivor Life Expectancy factor may be used:

RMD = Prior Dec. 31 balance ÷ IRS Joint Life factor

The calculator therefore asks you to enter the applicable Joint Life factor for this situation.

Understanding Your Results

Estimated RMD

This is the estimated minimum distribution produced by dividing the prior year-end balance by the applicable IRS distribution period.

The result is a withdrawal amount, not an estimate of the income tax you will owe. The tax treatment of a distribution can depend on the account, basis, and other tax circumstances.

IRS Distribution Period

A larger distribution-period factor produces a smaller RMD for the same account balance. As the applicable Uniform Lifetime denominator generally decreases with age, the required percentage of the account balance generally increases.

5-Year Projection

The projection uses the annual return assumption entered in the calculator to provide an estimate of future values. Actual investment returns can be higher, lower, or negative, so the projection should not be treated as a guaranteed future account balance or withdrawal amount.

Assumptions and Limitations

This calculator provides an estimate based on the information you enter and the applicable IRS distribution-period factor.

It does not fully model inherited retirement-account distribution schedules, every employer-plan exception, first-year RMD deferral choices, account aggregation rules, qualified charitable distributions, all Roth-account exceptions, or penalties for missed distributions.

The special younger-spouse calculation depends on entering the correct IRS Joint Life factor.

The 5-year projection depends on the annual return assumption you provide. Actual market returns are not guaranteed.

Employer retirement plans may also have plan-specific rules that affect when distributions begin. Verify the final required amount and deadline with your retirement-plan administrator, IRA custodian, tax professional, and current IRS guidance.

Current Rules / Data

2026 RMD Calculation

For a 2026 IRA RMD, IRS guidance generally uses the account balance at the end of December 31, 2025, divided by the applicable denominator for your age in 2026.

When RMDs Begin

Under IRS guidance applicable in 2026, IRA owners subject to the current starting-age rule generally begin RMDs at age 73. The first IRA RMD can generally be delayed until April 1 of the following year, while later annual RMDs are generally due by December 31. Employer-plan rules can allow a later starting date in certain situations.

SECURE 2.0 also provides for an applicable RMD age of 75 for later cohorts, so the correct starting age depends on the applicable law and the account owner's birth year.

Roth Accounts

Roth IRAs do not require lifetime RMDs for the original owner. Current IRS rules also exclude designated Roth accounts in retirement plans from lifetime RMD requirements while the employee is alive. Beneficiaries can still be subject to distribution requirements.

Missed RMDs

If a required amount is not distributed, an excise tax can apply to the shortfall. IRS guidance currently states a 25% tax, potentially reduced to 10% when the failure is corrected within the applicable correction period. This calculator does not calculate that tax.

Frequently Asked Questions

What account balance is used to calculate an RMD?

A standard annual RMD generally starts with the retirement account balance at the end of December 31 of the previous year. For a 2026 RMD, that generally means the December 31, 2025 balance.

Which IRS table does the calculator use?

Most account owners use the Uniform Lifetime Table. If your spouse is your sole beneficiary and is more than 10 years younger, the Joint and Last Survivor Life Expectancy Table may apply instead.

Why does a younger spouse affect the RMD?

The IRS provides a separate joint-life table for owners whose spouse is their sole beneficiary and is more than 10 years younger. That table uses both spouses' ages to determine the applicable distribution period.

Does the estimated annual return change my current RMD?

No. The current RMD calculation uses the prior year-end balance and applicable IRS distribution period. The annual return field is used for the calculator’s 5-year projection.

Can I withdraw more than the calculated RMD?

Yes. An RMD is generally the minimum required amount. However, withdrawing more than the required amount in one year does not reduce a future year's RMD requirement.

Does a Roth IRA have an RMD while the original owner is alive?

Generally, no. Roth IRA owners do not have lifetime RMD requirements. Beneficiaries can be subject to separate distribution rules.

What happens if I miss an RMD?

A federal excise tax may apply to the portion that was not distributed as required. Because correction rules and individual circumstances matter, check current IRS guidance if an RMD was missed.
