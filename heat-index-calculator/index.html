The Heat Index Calculator estimates how hot the air feels when air temperature and relative humidity are considered together. Enter the temperature and relative humidity to calculate the apparent, or “feels-like,” temperature using the National Weather Service Rothfusz regression.

What Is the Heat Index Calculator?

A heat index calculator combines air temperature with relative humidity to estimate apparent temperature. High humidity can make hot conditions feel warmer because moisture in the air reduces how efficiently sweat can evaporate from the skin.

This calculator is useful for checking hot-weather conditions during outdoor work, exercise, travel, sports, or other activities. The result is an environmental estimate, not a measurement of body temperature or an individual medical-risk assessment.

How to Use the Heat Index Calculator

Enter the two required values in this order:

  1. Air Temperature: Enter the air temperature. In the Rothfusz formula, temperature T is measured in degrees Fahrenheit (°F).
  2. Relative Humidity (%): Enter relative humidity as a percentage. For example, enter 50 for 50% humidity, not 0.50.

The calculator applies these values to the Rothfusz regression and returns the calculated heat index.

Formula / Calculation Method

Main Formula

The calculator uses the National Weather Service Rothfusz regression:

HI = −42.379 + 2.04901523T + 10.14333127RH − 0.22475541TRH − 0.00683783T² − 0.05481717RH² + 0.00122874T²RH + 0.00085282TRH² − 0.00000199T²RH²

This regression was developed to approximate apparent temperature from air temperature and relative humidity.

Variables

Symbol Meaning Unit
HI Calculated heat index °F
T Air temperature °F
RH Relative humidity %

Relative humidity is entered as a percentage value, such as 40, 60, or 80.

Calculation Sequence

The calculator follows this calculation process:

  1. Read the air temperature as T.
  2. Read relative humidity as RH.
  3. Calculate the squared terms T² and RH².
  4. Calculate the interaction terms such as T × RH, T² × RH, and T × RH².
  5. Substitute all values into the Rothfusz regression.
  6. Combine the terms to obtain the heat index in degrees Fahrenheit.

The calculation described here is the Rothfusz regression itself. The NWS operational heat-index procedure can also use additional adjustments or a simpler equation under certain temperature and humidity conditions.

Worked Example

Suppose:

Inputs

  • Air temperature: 95°F
  • Relative humidity: 50%

Step 1: Substitute the Values

Using T = 95 and RH = 50:

HI = −42.379 + 2.04901523(95) + 10.14333127(50) − 0.22475541(95)(50) − 0.00683783(95²) − 0.05481717(50²) + 0.00122874(95²)(50) + 0.00085282(95)(50²) − 0.00000199(95²)(50²)

Step 2: Calculate the Terms

The main calculated terms are approximately:

  • 2.04901523 × 95 = 194.6564
  • 10.14333127 × 50 = 507.1666
  • −0.22475541 × 95 × 50 = −1067.5882
  • −0.00683783 × 95² = −61.7114
  • −0.05481717 × 50² = −137.0429
  • 0.00122874 × 95² × 50 = 554.4689
  • 0.00085282 × 95 × 50² = 202.5448
  • −0.00000199 × 95² × 50² = −44.8994

Step 3: Combine the Terms

Adding these values with the constant −42.379 gives:

HI ≈ 105.2158°F

Final Result

The calculated heat index is approximately 105.22°F.

This means that an air temperature of 95°F with 50% relative humidity produces an apparent temperature of about 105°F under the assumptions represented by the formula.

Understanding Your Results

The main result is the heat index, expressed as an apparent temperature.

For example, a result of 105°F does not mean the measured air temperature has increased to 105°F. It means the combination of temperature and humidity can produce heat conditions that feel approximately like that apparent temperature.

Heat index should not be interpreted as body temperature, a diagnosis, or a personal medical threshold. Individual heat response can differ, and official local heat alerts should be followed when dangerous conditions are present.

Assumptions and Limitations

The Rothfusz regression has several important limitations.

Hot-weather range: The National Weather Service states that the Rothfusz regression is not appropriate when temperature and humidity conditions would produce a heat index below about 80°F. Its operational procedure uses another method for lower values.

Extreme combinations: The regression is not valid for extreme combinations of temperature and relative humidity outside the data range used to develop the underlying heat-index relationship.

Shade and light wind: Official NWS heat-index values are designed for shady, light-wind conditions. Exposure to direct sunlight can increase the apparent heat effect by as much as about 15°F.

Not a personal medical prediction: The calculated value does not predict an individual's body temperature, hydration level, or likelihood of developing a heat-related illness.

Formula scope: This calculator uses air temperature and relative humidity. Other environmental or personal factors are not part of the Rothfusz equation shown above.

Current NWS Guidance

As of September 2026, National Weather Service resources continue to describe heat index as an apparent-temperature measure based on air temperature and relative humidity. The NWS also continues to note that published heat-index values are based on shady, light-wind conditions and that full sun can make conditions feel substantially hotter.

For real-world heat safety decisions, use current local National Weather Service forecasts, advisories, watches, and warnings rather than relying only on a calculated heat-index value.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does the heat index measure?

The heat index estimates how hot conditions feel when air temperature and relative humidity are considered together. It is also called apparent or “feels-like” temperature.

What formula does this calculator use?

It uses the National Weather Service Rothfusz regression, which combines temperature in degrees Fahrenheit with relative humidity expressed as a percentage.

Is relative humidity entered as a decimal?

No. Relative humidity is used as a percentage in the formula. For 60% relative humidity, RH = 60, not 0.60.

Is heat index the same as air temperature?

No. Air temperature is the measured atmospheric temperature. Heat index combines temperature and humidity to estimate apparent heat.

Is the Rothfusz formula accurate at low temperatures?

The NWS states that the regression is not appropriate when conditions produce a heat index below about 80°F. Its complete operational method uses a simpler calculation for lower conditions.

Does the calculator account for direct sunlight?

Direct sunlight is not a variable in the Rothfusz equation shown here. NWS guidance notes that full sunshine can increase the apparent heat effect by up to about 15°F compared with shaded conditions.

Can I use the result to determine my risk of heat illness?

The result provides an environmental heat estimate, not an individualized medical assessment. Personal risk can depend on factors that are not included in this calculation, so current local heat warnings and appropriate safety guidance should also be considered.
