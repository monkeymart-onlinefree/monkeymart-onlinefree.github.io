Molecular Weight Calculator

Use the Molecular Weight Calculator to calculate the molar mass of a chemical formula from the standard atomic weights of its elements. Enter a case-sensitive chemical formula or select a common compound to get the calculated molecular weight.

What Is the Molecular Weight Calculator?

The Molecular Weight Calculator determines the molar mass of a compound by counting each element in its chemical formula, multiplying that count by the element’s standard atomic weight, and adding the results.

The calculator can be useful for chemistry calculations involving compounds, solution preparation, stoichiometry, and conversions between mass and amount of substance. It also supports grouped formulas and common hydrates.

IUPAC defines molar mass as mass divided by amount of substance and commonly expresses it in g/mol or kg/mol. When g/mol is used, its numerical value is equal to the relative molecular mass for the same composition.

How to Use the Molecular Weight Calculator

Formula

Enter the chemical formula of the compound.

Chemical formulas are case-sensitive, so element symbols must use the correct capitalization. For example, Co represents cobalt, while CO represents carbon and oxygen.

The calculator can process formulas containing grouped elements, such as:

Ca(OH)2

Common Compound

Use the Common compound option to select a preset compound instead of manually entering its formula.

After the formula is entered or selected, the calculator determines its molar mass from the atomic weights and the number of atoms represented in the formula.

Formula / Calculation Method

Main Formula

The calculator uses:

M ≈ Σ(nᵢ × Aᵢ)

where the contributions from every element in the chemical formula are added together.

Variables

  • M = calculated molar mass, typically expressed in g/mol
  • nᵢ = number of atoms of element i in the chemical formula
  • Aᵢ = standard atomic weight used for element i
  • Σ = sum of the contributions from all elements

Calculation Sequence

  1. Read the case-sensitive chemical formula.
  2. Identify each element in the formula.
  3. Apply subscripts and grouped-formula multipliers.
  4. Count the total number of atoms of each element.
  5. Multiply each element count by its atomic weight.
  6. Add all element contributions.
  7. Return the calculated molar mass.

For grouped formulas, a multiplier outside parentheses applies to every element inside that group.

Worked Example

Calculate the molecular weight of calcium hydroxide:

Ca(OH)2

Inputs

Formula: Ca(OH)2

Using practical abridged atomic-weight values:

  • Ca ≈ 40.078
  • O ≈ 15.999
  • H ≈ 1.008

Step 1: Count the Atoms

Ca(OH)2 contains:

  • 1 calcium atom
  • 2 oxygen atoms
  • 2 hydrogen atoms

Step 2: Calculate Each Contribution

Calcium:

1 × 40.078 = 40.078

Oxygen:

2 × 15.999 = 31.998

Hydrogen:

2 × 1.008 = 2.016

Step 3: Add the Contributions

M = 40.078 + 31.998 + 2.016
M = 74.092 g/mol

Final Result

The calculated molar mass of Ca(OH)2 is approximately:

74.092 g/mol

The final digits can vary slightly when a different valid atomic-weight table or rounding convention is used.

Understanding Your Results

The main calculated result is the compound’s molar mass.

A result such as:

74.092 g/mol

means that one mole of formula units represented by that chemical formula has a calculated mass of approximately 74.092 grams when using the atomic weights applied by the calculator.

The page is called a Molecular Weight Calculator, but “molecular weight” is commonly used as a synonym for relative molecular mass. Strictly, relative molecular mass is dimensionless, while molar mass has units such as g/mol. Their numerical values are equal when molar mass is expressed in g/mol.

Assumptions and Limitations

The calculator uses practical abridged atomic weights suitable for general chemical calculations. It does not calculate isotope-specific exact masses.

Standard atomic weights can vary because the isotopic composition of some elements is not identical in every natural material. CIAAW therefore reports intervals or uncertainties for a number of elements rather than treating every atomic weight as an absolutely fixed value.

The entered chemical formula must also correctly represent the intended compound. Because formulas are case-sensitive, incorrect capitalization can change the elements being interpreted.

Results can differ slightly from another textbook, laboratory system, or calculator because of differences in the atomic-weight table and the number of decimal places retained.

Current Atomic-Weight Data

The Commission on Isotopic Abundances and Atomic Weights (CIAAW) currently publishes its Abridged Standard Atomic Weights 2024 table for practical calculations. The table uses abridged recommended values and uncertainties, while the full standard atomic-weight table provides additional detail for elements whose natural isotopic composition varies.

CIAAW revised the recommended standard atomic weights of gadolinium, lutetium, and zirconium in 2024. This illustrates why atomic-weight reference data should be reviewed periodically even though the calculation method itself remains unchanged.

Frequently Asked Questions

What formula does the Molecular Weight Calculator use?

It uses:

M ≈ Σ(nᵢ × Aᵢ)

Each element’s atomic weight is multiplied by the number of atoms of that element, and all contributions are added.

Is molecular weight the same as molar mass?

They are closely related but technically different quantities. Relative molecular mass, sometimes called molecular weight, is dimensionless. Molar mass has units such as g/mol. When molar mass is expressed in g/mol, the numerical values are equal.

Why is the chemical formula case-sensitive?

Element symbols depend on capitalization. For example, Co is the symbol for cobalt, while C and O represent carbon and oxygen separately.

Does the calculator support parentheses?

Yes. The calculator supports grouped chemical formulas. A multiplier after a group applies to the elements inside that group, such as the 2 in Ca(OH)2.

Can it calculate hydrated compounds?

The calculator supports common hydrate formulas. The hydrate components are included in the total atom count before the molar-mass contributions are summed.

Why might my result differ from another source?

Different sources may use different atomic-weight editions, abridged values, or rounding precision. Natural isotopic variation can also produce ranges for the standard atomic weights of some elements.

Does this calculate exact isotopic mass?

No. The calculator uses practical standard atomic-weight values. Isotope-specific exact-mass calculations require isotope-specific atomic masses rather than ordinary standard atomic weights.
