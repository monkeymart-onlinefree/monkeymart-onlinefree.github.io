What Is the Wind Chill Calculator?

The wind chill calculator estimates how cold exposed skin feels when cold air and wind act together. It uses the current U.S. National Weather Service wind chill equation and enforces the conditions under which that equation is officially defined.

How to Use the Wind Chill Calculator

Enter air temperature and wind speed, choose the units, and calculate. The tool converts inputs to Fahrenheit and miles per hour internally, applies the NWS equation when its validity conditions are met, and reports both Fahrenheit and Celsius.

Wind Chill Calculator Formula

WCT(°F) = 35.74 + 0.6215T − 35.75V^0.16 + 0.4275T·V^0.16

Formula Variables

T is air temperature in °F and V is wind speed in mph. The NWS defines the index for T ≤ 50°F and V > 3 mph.

Calculation Example

At 20°F with a 15 mph wind, the equation produces a wind chill of about 6°F.

Understanding Your Results

Wind chill is an apparent temperature, not the thermometer reading. A larger difference between air temperature and wind chill indicates faster heat loss from exposed skin under the model assumptions.

Important Factors

Wind speed, air temperature, sunshine, clothing, moisture, activity, and exposure time all affect human comfort and cold stress.

Limitations

The NWS formula assumes a standard exposed-face model and no solar heating. It should not be used outside the official temperature and wind-speed range, and it is not a medical diagnosis or individualized safety limit.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the wind chill formula valid?

The U.S. National Weather Service defines wind chill temperature for air temperatures at or below 50°F and wind speeds above 3 mph.

Can wind chill make objects colder than the air temperature?

Wind can make exposed people and objects lose heat faster, but wind chill is an apparent-temperature index for exposed skin, not a new physical air temperature.

Does sunshine affect wind chill?

Yes. The NWS notes that bright sunshine can make it feel warmer than the standard wind-chill estimate because the formula assumes no solar impact.
