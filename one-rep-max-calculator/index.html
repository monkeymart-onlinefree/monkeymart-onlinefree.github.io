The One Rep Max Calculator estimates the maximum weight you may be able to lift for one repetition. Enter the weight lifted, choose the unit, enter 1–10 repetitions, and select the Epley, Brzycki, or Lombardi formula to calculate an estimated 1RM.

What Is the One Rep Max Calculator?

A one-repetition maximum, or 1RM, represents maximal strength for a specific exercise. This calculator estimates that value from a submaximal set instead of requiring a direct maximal attempt.

It is useful for lifters, coaches, and people using percentage-based resistance training. The result is an estimate for the exercise and performance entered, not a guaranteed weight you can safely lift once.

How to Use the One Rep Max Calculator

  1. Weight lifted: Enter the weight you successfully lifted during the set.
  2. Unit: Select the unit used for the entered weight. The estimated 1RM remains in the same unit.
  3. Repetitions: Enter the number of completed repetitions from 1 to 10.
  4. Formula: Choose Epley, Brzycki, or Lombardi.
  5. Review the estimated one-rep max and any percentage-based supporting values shown by the calculator.

Using no more than 10 repetitions is consistent with research showing that prediction accuracy can decrease as repetition counts increase; one study of bench press and leg press found 5RM data predicted measured 1RM more accurately than 10RM or 20RM data.

Formula / Calculation Method

The calculator uses the selected formula to convert the lifted weight and repetition count into an estimated 1RM. Epley, Brzycki, and Lombardi belong to the group of repetitions-to-fatigue equations studied for predicting maximal strength.

Epley Formula

1RM = w × (1 + r ÷ 30)

Brzycki Formula

1RM = w ÷ (1.0278 − 0.0278 × r)

Lombardi Formula

1RM = w × r^0.10

Variables

  • 1RM = estimated one-repetition maximum, in the same weight unit as the input
  • w = weight lifted
  • r = repetitions completed, from 1 to 10

Calculation Sequence

The calculator first reads the entered weight and repetition count. It then applies the selected Epley, Brzycki, or Lombardi equation.

Because the formulas use the entered weight multiplied or divided by dimensionless values, the resulting 1RM remains in the same unit as the weight input.

Percentage-Based Supporting Loads

When the calculator displays a load based on a percentage of the estimated 1RM, the calculation is:

Percentage Load = Estimated 1RM × (Percentage ÷ 100)

For example, a displayed percentage represented by P is calculated as:

Load at P% = 1RM × P ÷ 100

The exact percentages shown by the calculator should be interpreted as relative training loads based on the selected formula's estimated 1RM.

Worked Example

Suppose a lifter completes 5 repetitions with a weight of 100 and selects the Epley formula.

Inputs

  • Weight lifted: 100
  • Repetitions: 5
  • Formula: Epley
  • Unit: the same unit used for the entered weight

Step 1

Start with the Epley equation:

1RM = w × (1 + r ÷ 30)

Substitute the values:

1RM = 100 × (1 + 5 ÷ 30)

Step 2

Calculate the repetition adjustment:

5 ÷ 30 = 0.166666...
1 + 0.166666... = 1.166666...

Step 3

Multiply by the lifted weight:

100 × 1.166666... = 116.666...

Final Result

Estimated 1RM ≈ 116.67

The result uses the same weight unit as the original 100-unit input. Choosing Brzycki or Lombardi can produce a different estimate because each formula models the relationship between repetitions and maximum strength differently.

Understanding Your Results

Estimated 1RM

The main result is the predicted maximum load for one repetition of the same exercise. It should be treated as a strength estimate rather than proof that the calculated load can be completed safely.

Percentage-Based Loads

Supporting percentage values express training loads relative to the estimated 1RM. They can make it easier to translate an estimated maximum into lighter working loads.

The relationship between repetitions and percentage of 1RM is not identical for everyone. Research involving thousands of repetition-to-failure tests found substantial individual variation and examined differences related to exercise, age, sex, and training status.

Estimated vs. Directly Tested 1RM

A calculated 1RM and a directly tested 1RM are different measurements. Direct 1RM testing has generally shown good-to-excellent test-retest reliability across studies, but a prediction equation still introduces estimation error.

As of March 2026, the American College of Sports Medicine's updated resistance-training position stand reports that heavier loads of at least about 80% of 1RM can enhance voluntary strength in healthy adults. This provides context for percentage-based training, but it does not mean every person should train at the same percentage or attempt a true maximum.

Assumptions and Limitations

The calculator estimates 1RM from weight and repetitions; it does not directly measure maximal strength.

Results can vary because of exercise selection, lifting technique, fatigue, training experience, individual muscular endurance, and how close the entered set was to the lifter's actual repetition limit.

The calculator accepts 1–10 repetitions. Higher-repetition sets are not part of this calculator's supported input range, and research indicates that prediction accuracy can decline as repetition counts rise.

The estimate is exercise-specific. An estimated 1RM for one movement should not be treated as the 1RM for another exercise.

At 1 repetition, the formulas do not behave identically. Brzycki and Lombardi return the entered weight, while the implemented Epley equation calculates:

w × (1 + 1 ÷ 30) = 1.03333... × w

This is a mathematical consequence of the Epley formula used by the calculator.

A formula cannot determine whether a maximal attempt is appropriate or safe for a particular person. Fatigue, form, equipment, health status, and supervision still matter. People who are inexperienced with heavy lifting or have relevant health concerns should not interpret an estimated 1RM as clearance to perform an unsupervised maximal attempt.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does 1RM mean?

1RM means one-repetition maximum. It represents the maximum load associated with one successful repetition of a particular exercise.

Which 1RM formula does this calculator use?

You can select one of three formulas: Epley, Brzycki, or Lombardi. The calculator applies only the formula you choose to the entered weight and repetitions.

Why do Epley, Brzycki, and Lombardi give different results?

Each equation models the relationship between repetitions and maximal strength differently. Therefore, the same weight and repetition count can produce different estimated 1RM values. Prediction accuracy can also differ by exercise and repetition range.

Is an estimated 1RM the same as a tested 1RM?

No. An estimated 1RM is calculated from a submaximal set. A tested 1RM is measured by actually completing a maximal single repetition under a testing protocol.

Why is the repetition range limited to 1–10?

Lower-repetition sets are generally more suitable for estimating maximal strength than high-repetition sets. Research on multiple-repetition testing found that prediction accuracy decreased as the tested repetition count increased and recommended no more than 10 repetitions for the linear prediction equations examined.

Why can the Epley result be higher than my entered weight when I enter one repetition?

The implemented Epley equation is 1RM = w × (1 + r ÷ 30). When r = 1, it becomes approximately 1.0333 × w, so the estimate is about 3.33% above the entered weight. Brzycki and Lombardi return the entered weight when one repetition is used.

Can I use the percentage results as exact training targets?

They are better treated as reference values. The number of repetitions a person can perform at a given percentage of 1RM varies between people and exercises, so percentage-based loads should not be interpreted as universal rep guarantees.

    Current resistance-training guidance used for percentage-of-1RM training context in healthy adults.
